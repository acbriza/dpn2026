\abstract{\textbf{Purpose:} Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is the most prevalent polyneuropathy among Filipinos with diabetes, yet remains underdiagnosed in up to 81\% of affected patients. Definitive diagnosis through nerve conduction study (NCS) is costly and geographically inaccessible in many settings. This study investigates the use of machine learning (ML) and counterfactual explanation techniques as a pre-diagnostic screening tool to identify patients with a high probability of DPN and prioritize them for NCS referral.

\textbf{Methods:} A total of 190 adult Filipino patients diagnosedacnoquDov100acnoquDov100 with Diabetes Mellitus were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Quezon City, Philippines, between 2022 and 2023. Patients underwent Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI) assessment, standard neurological examination, Sudoscan sudomotor testing, and lower extremity nerve conduction study. Patients were classified as Confirmed DPN (n = 131) or Unconfirmed DPN (n = 59) based on the 2009 Toronto consensus criteria. Fourteen classification algorithms were evaluated for baseline performance using default parameters using repeated stratified k-fold cross-validation (10 repeats × 4 folds) across twelve feature subsets. The best-performing model underwent Bayesian hyperparameter optimization. Post-hoc interpretability was provided through CatBoost feature importance, SHAP analysis, and counterfactual explanations generated using the DiCE (Diverse Counterfactual Explanations) framework.

\textbf{Results:} CatBoost achieved the highest initial Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) among 14 algorithms. After optimization across repeated cross-validation, it achieved an AUPRC of 0.885 (95\% CI: 0.868--0.902), representing a 29\% relative improvement over the no-skill baseline, while obtaining a mean sensitivity of 0.924 (95\% CI: 0.904--0.944). Feature set ablation revealed that neurological examination features produced the greatest performance degradation upon removal, and that the combination of Sudoscan and neurological examination findings maintained performance close to the full feature set. CatBoost feature importance and SHAP analyses converged consistently across all four cross-validation folds on decreased ankle reflex, decreased vibration sensation, Sudoscan-derived sudomotor parameters, and diabetes duration as the dominant predictors. However, while these features ranked highest in both importance and SHAP analyses, they represent measurements of neuropathic state rather than modifiable intervention targets, limiting their direct clinical actionability. Counterfactual analysis bridged this gap by identifying the minimal changes to modifiable patient features that would alter the model's predicted DPN classification: HbA1c modification emerged as the dominant actionable pathway in 91 of 97 generated counterfactuals (93.8\%), with dyslipidemia management and chronic kidney disease status identified as secondary intervention targets, appearing in 19.6\% and 23.7\% of counterfactuals respectively.

\textbf{Conclusions:} A CatBoost-based ML model trained on non-NCS clinical features demonstrated high sensitivity for DPN pre-diagnostic screening in a Philippine clinical population, with performance competitive with published benchmarks. The multi-lens analytical approach --- spanning feature ablation, feature importance, SHAP, and counterfactual analysis --- revealed that while neurological examination and Sudoscan features are the strongest predictors of DPN risk, counterfactual explanations are necessary to translate model behavior into clinically actionable guidance. These findings support the potential utility of ML-assisted DPN screening in resource-constrained settings where NCS is unavailable or inaccessible, while identifying glycemic optimization, dyslipidemia management, and nephroprotective interventions as the most actionable modifiable risk pathways. External validation in multi-center, geographically diverse cohorts is necessary before clinical deployment.}

\section{Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{introduction}

% The Introduction section expands on the background of the work (some overlap with the Abstract is acceptable). The introduction should not include subheadings.


Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is the most common type of polyneuropathy affecting 42-58\% of Filipinos \cite{ropper2019adams, araneta2006hyperglycemia, dagang2016validation}.
Characterized by progressive nerve damage, DPN often presents with symptoms such as pain, numbness, and loss of sensation in the extremities, particularly the feet. Beyond its direct impact on quality of life, DPN is a major risk factor for foot ulcers, infections, and lower-limb amputations, contributing significantly to healthcare burden.

Despite being associated with reduced quality of life and debilitating complications,
DPN is underdiagnosed in up to 81\% of patients \cite{vinik2012neuropathy, ziegler2018painful}. Hurdles in the early diagnosis of DPN includes the insidious progression, inconsistent disease presentation and the need for complicated equipment for definitive diagnosis \cite{vinik2012neuropathy, ziegler2018painful, casellini2013sudoscan}.

Skin biopsy with quantitation of intraepidermal nerve fiber density is still the gold standard in detecting small fiber neuropathy, while sudorimetry is a rapidly evolving technology that can easily be integrated into clinical practice. The development of such technologies like the Sudoscan allows noninvasive and easy-to-perform tests for detection of small fiber neuropathy based on reverse iontophoresis and chronoamperometry to measure sudomotor function \cite{mayaudon:2010newsimple, vinik2015newage}.

Early detection and management of DPN are therefore critical, yet remain challenging due to the multifactorial nature of the disease and the heterogeneity of patient profiles. In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool for modeling complex, nonlinear relationships in medical data. By leveraging large clinical datasets, ML algorithms can identify patterns and interactions among risk factors that may not be readily apparent through traditional statistical approaches. 

Numerous studies have developed ML models with good discriminatory performance (AUCs ranging from 0.64 to 0.93), employing diverse algorithms such as random forest, XGBoost, and gradient boosting. Models often incorporate clinical, biochemical, and even traditional Chinese medicine features, enhancing predictive accuracy and relevance \cite{ma2025machine,jiang2024multifeature,cui2023study,lian2023Study,Wei2024construction}. Internal validation methods like cross-validation are widely used, and some studies report calibration and decision curve analyses, supporting clinical utility \cite{cui2023study}.

Despite the  predictive performance, many ML models—particularly ensemble and deep learning methods—are often criticized as “black boxes,” limiting their adoption in clinical settings where interpretability and trust are essential. To address this limitation, the field of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has gained increasing attention. XAI techniques aim to make model predictions more transparent by providing insights into how input features influence outcomes. Methods such as feature importance scores, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations), and partial dependence plots have been widely used to offer global and local interpretability. 

The application of XAI techniques such as SHAP, LIME, and Anchor explanations is increasingly common, providing transparent insights into feature importance and model decisions, which is critical for clinical trust and adoption \cite{wu2025prognostic,jiang2024multifeature, pandey2024decoding, wu2024interpretable, sheikh2025predicting}. Integration of XAI with multimodal data enables identification of key risk factors and supports personalized treatment recommendations \cite{nguyen2024interpretable, alHudaibi2025explainable}. Some frameworks incorporate human-in-the-loop feedback to enhance interpretability and clinical relevance \cite{lalwani2025generative}. While these approaches help elucidate model behavior, they primarily describe associations rather than actionable insights, which are crucial for clinical decision-making.

Counterfactual explanations represent a more recent and clinically meaningful approach within XAI. Instead of merely explaining why a prediction was made, counterfactuals answer “what-if” questions by identifying minimal changes to a patient’s features that would lead to a different predicted outcome. For example, a counterfactual explanation might suggest how modifying certain clinical variables—such as glycemic control or body mass index—could reduce a patient’s predicted risk of developing DPN. This aligns closely with the needs of personalized medicine, where interventions are tailored to individual patients based on modifiable risk factors. Several methodological approaches for generating counterfactual explanations have been proposed in the literature. One of the most popular is the DiCE framework \cite{mothilal2020explaining}, an optimization-based method, which formulate counterfactual generation as a constrained optimization problem that seeks minimal changes to input features while achieving a desired prediction;

The integration of machine learning and counterfactual explanations offers a promising framework for both the prediction and interpretation of diabetic peripheral neuropathy. By combining high predictive accuracy with actionable, patient-specific insights, this approach has the potential to support clinicians in early diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment planning. Moreover, it bridges the gap between advanced computational models and practical clinical utility, fostering greater trust and adoption of AI-driven tools in healthcare. 

This study aims to investigate the application of machine learning models in conjunction with counterfactual explanation techniques for the prediction and interpretation of diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) risk, thereby contributing to the growing body of research on interpretable and actionable artificial intelligence in medicine. The study also examines the potential insights derived from incorporating nerve conduction study results and Sudoscan measurements—modalities that are comparatively underrepresented in many AI-based DPN studies, which often rely primarily on routine clinical and demographic variables. Furthermore, by utilizing data collected within the Philippine clinical context, this work enhances the geographic diversity of current studies and provides context-specific insights into the application of explainable AI for DPN.

\section{Data Collection}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{datacollection}

A total of 201 adult Filipino patients diagnosed with Diabetes Mellitus (DM) were recruited
between 2022 and 2023. Subjects with limb amputation, gangrenous/ necrotic foot, with implanted
cardiac pacemakers or defibrillators, and patients with other known causes of polyneuropathy were
excluded. Remaining 190 qualified subjects underwent a series of detailed assessment comprising of
Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI) test, serial neurologic tests, and lower extremity NCV
examination to detect the presence of DPN. Subjects were then classified based on the 2009 Toronto
consensus criteria as confirmed, probable, possible or no DPN \cite{tesfaye2010diabetic}.  Of the 190 enrolled patients, 3 patients were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete data, resulting in a final dataset of 187 patients.  As the purpose of the study is prescreening for DPN, the study focused on the binary classification of patients into two groups: Confirmed DPN and Unconfirmed DPN. This resulted in a dataset of 131 patients with Confirmed DPN and 56 patients with Unconfirmed DPN.  The patient flow and classification breakdown is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:patient_flow_classification}.

Neuropathic symptoms were documented by subjectively asking patients for presence of any
distal extremity numbness, paresthesia, or pain, and by asking them to answer the Filipino validated
version of MNSI \cite{dagang2016validation}. Abnormal neurologic signs were detected using the standard 10g Semmes Weinstein monofilament examination (SWME) and standard 128-Hz clanging tuning fork tests \cite{feng2009semmes,oyer2007quantitative}. Additional examination for pinprick sensation was done using the same criteria for SWME. Achilles tendon reflex testing was also done and dichotomized subjects as either normal or hypo/areflexive. All neurological examinations were conducted by a single trained physician.

Nerve conduction studies were performed at a stable skin temperature of 32°C and a room
temperature of 24°C using Nihon Kohen EMG equipment with a filter width of 20 Hz to 3 kHz, amplifier
setting at 200 $\mu$V/div, and sweep speed of 10 ms/div. 

The following nerve attributes were measured: 
\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0em 
\item Sural sensory nerve action potentials (SNAP) and conduction velocities (CV)
\item Superficial peroneal SNAP and CV
\item Tibial motor nerve compound muscle action potentials (CMAP), CV, distal latencies (DL) and
F-wave latencies
\end{itemize}

All NCV testing was done by a single trained technician on the same day as other
neurological examinations. Finally, sudomotor testing using Sudoscan was performed on each participant. Subjects placed palms of their hands and the soles of their feet on stainless steel electrodes and an incremental low direct voltage (<4V) was applied for about 2 minutes. The device produced an average electrochemical skin conductance (ESC) result for both hands and feet which is based on stimulation of sweat glands by the low-level voltage, allowing evidence of sweat dysfunction \cite{garcia2022detection}.

The full list of features, their groupings, description and code are shown in Table~\ref{tab:clinical_data}.

This study was approved by the local Institutional Ethics Review Board and all subjects gave their written consent prior to participating.

\subsection{Machine Learning Modeling}
%*************************************
In this section, we describe the machine learning models used, the model and feature-set selection procedure, the safeguards against data leakage and against the effects of a small, imbalanced sample, and the nested-cross-validation hyperparameter optimization and explainability procedures applied to the selected model.

\subsubsection{Models}
%=====================
\label{sec:models}

A diverse set of machine learning algorithms was evaluated in this study using default or standard parameter settings to ensure a consistent and unbiased baseline comparison. These models can be broadly grouped according to their underlying learning paradigms:

\textit{(a) Linear and Probabilistic Models}.
This group includes Logistic Regression, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGDClassifier), Naive Bayes (GaussianNB), and Linear Support Vector Machine (Linear SVM). These models are generally characterized by strong interpretability and computational efficiency. Logistic Regression and Linear SVM are effective in high-dimensional spaces and provide robust linear decision boundaries. LDA and QDA model class-conditional distributions, with QDA allowing nonlinear decision boundaries through class-specific covariance matrices. Naive Bayes is particularly efficient and performs well under conditional independence assumptions, while SGDClassifier enables scalable optimization for large datasets.

\textit{(b) Distance-Based and Instance-Based Learning}.
The k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) algorithm falls under this category. It is a non-parametric method that makes predictions based on the proximity of data points in feature space. Its strength lies in its simplicity and ability to capture complex decision boundaries without explicit model training, although it may be sensitive to feature scaling and noise.

\textit{(c) Tree-Based and Ensemble Methods}.
This group includes Decision Tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees, and Gradient Boosting. Decision Trees are intuitive and capable of capturing nonlinear relationships and feature interactions. Random Forest and Extra Trees extend this capability through ensemble learning, improving generalization and reducing variance via bagging and randomized feature selection. Gradient Boosting builds models sequentially to correct previous errors, often achieving strong predictive performance.

\textit{(d) Advanced Gradient Boosting Frameworks}.
More sophisticated implementations of boosting are represented by XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. These algorithms are optimized for efficiency and performance on structured/tabular data. XGBoost incorporates regularization and efficient handling of sparse data; LightGBM employs histogram-based learning and leaf-wise tree growth for speed and scalability; CatBoost is particularly effective in handling categorical variables and mitigating overfitting through ordered boosting techniques.

\textit{(e) Kernel-Based Models}.
The RBF Support Vector Machine (RBF SVM) extends linear SVM by applying a radial basis function kernel, enabling the modeling of nonlinear decision boundaries. This makes it powerful for complex datasets where linear separability is not sufficient.

\textit{(f) Baseline Model}.
A Naive classifier that predicts the majority class was included as a baseline to contextualize the performance of all other models.

Overall, this diverse selection of models allows for a comprehensive evaluation across linear, nonlinear, probabilistic, and ensemble-based learning paradigms, each with distinct strengths in terms of interpretability, flexibility, robustness, and predictive power.

\subsubsection{Model and Feature Set Selection}
%==============================================
\label{sec:modelselection}

Given the relatively limited number of unconfirmed cases in the dataset, a robust evaluation strategy was used to generate reliable performance estimates for the model-selection step. Fourteen algorithms (Section~\ref{sec:models}) were benchmarked with default/standard hyperparameters under a repeated stratified k-fold cross-validation scheme -- 4 folds $\times$ 10 repetitions, for 40 validation runs per model -- against each of twelve feature sets (Table~\ref{tab:feature_sets}), giving $14 \times 12 \times 40$ scored runs in total. Stratification ensures that each fold preserves the class distribution of the dataset, which is particularly important given 57 Unconfirmed patients; repetition reduces the variance of the resulting performance estimate by averaging results across multiple random partitions.

To assess the contribution of different feature subsets, all fourteen models were first trained on the complete feature set to establish baseline performance, then retrained on eleven further feature sets that systematically remove or isolate each feature group defined in Table~\ref{tab:clinical_data}-A (Table~\ref{tab:feature_sets}). This ablation strategy identifies which feature groups contribute most to predictive performance and quantifies the standalone value of the Sudoscan and nerve-conduction-study modalities.

For every model--feature-set combination, the 40 per-run scores of each metric were summarised by their mean; models and feature sets were then ranked by mean Area Under the Precision--Recall Curve (\texttt{sort\_by: auprc} in the selection configuration), since AUPRC is the more informative summary of discrimination under class imbalance (Section~\ref{sec:classimbalance}). CatBoost on the full feature set attained the highest mean AUPRC and was carried forward to hyperparameter optimization (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}); the full model $\times$ feature-set comparison (mean AUPRC across all fourteen models and twelve feature sets) and the run-to-run distribution of AUPRC for the selected model and feature set are reported in the Discussion.

In addition, multicollinearity among the 22 candidate predictors was addressed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF): features exceeding a VIF of 5 were dropped to construct the reduced ``NoCol'' feature set (Table~\ref{tab:feature_sets}), aimed at assessing model stability and interpretability under reduced collinearity.

\subsubsection{Performance Metrics}
%==================================
For each of the 40 validation runs, nine metrics were recorded: accuracy, precision, sensitivity (recall), specificity, the Youden index, F1, F2, ROC-AUC, and AUPRC. Sensitivity and specificity jointly express the clinical trade-off of a pre-diagnostic screening tool, while AUPRC -- the metric used to rank models and feature sets (Section~\ref{sec:modelselection}) -- was prioritised over ROC-AUC because, at a 69.5\%/30.5\% class split, ROC-AUC can look favourable even when precision on the minority (Unconfirmed) class is poor, whereas AUPRC is sensitive to exactly that failure mode.

To further investigate the predictive value of specific diagnostic modalities, models were also trained exclusively on nerve conduction study (NCS) features and Sudoscan features, respectively. This allows for a focused evaluation of their standalone predictive capabilities and their potential role in clinical decision support.

\subsubsection{Preventing Data Leakage}
%======================================
\label{sec:leakage}

Every stage of the pipeline enforces a strict separation between the data used to fit or tune a model and the data used to evaluate it, so that reported performance is not optimistically biased:

\begin{itemize}
    \itemsep0em
    \item \textit{Selection.} The 40 repeated-CV runs for each model--feature-set pair each fit on a training fold and score on a disjoint test fold; no test fold is ever seen during fitting.
    \item \textit{Hyperparameter optimization.} The nested cross-validation described in Section~\ref{sec:hpo} confines hyperparameter search, refitting, and decision-threshold selection entirely to each outer iteration's training fold; the corresponding outer test fold is touched exactly once, after tuning and thresholding are complete, purely to record performance. A runtime assertion verifies, for every repeat, that the four outer test folds jointly and exactly tile all 187 patients -- so pooled outer-test performance is computed over the whole cohort without double-counting or omission, and without any patient's test-fold prediction coming from a model that trained on them.
    \item \textit{Explainability and counterfactual generation.} Both stages reuse the outer-training-fold models and fold-specific thresholds already fitted and selected during optimization (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}); they never refit against, or select a threshold from, an outer test fold.
\end{itemize}

This separation is what licenses treating outer-test performance, and the SHAP/feature-importance/discrimination/calibration diagnostics computed downstream of it, as out-of-sample estimates rather than measurements of how well the model fits data it has already seen.

\subsubsection{Handling Class Imbalance and a Small Sample Size}
%===============================================================
\label{sec:classimbalance}

The analysed cohort is small by machine-learning standards (187 patients, 22 candidate predictors) and imbalanced (130 Confirmed vs.\ 57 Unconfirmed, 69.5\%/30.5\%). Two complementary strategies address this, and a third common strategy -- synthetic minority oversampling -- was deliberately not used.

Repeated stratified cross-validation (Sections~\ref{sec:modelselection} and~\ref{sec:hpo}) is the primary defence against the variance a small $n$ introduces into any single performance estimate: stratification keeps the 69.5\%/30.5\% split intact in every fold, and repetition (10 repeats at the selection stage, 10 at the optimization stage) averages out the sampling variability of any one particular partition of 187 patients into folds.

Class imbalance itself is handled inside the model rather than by resampling the data. CatBoost's \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} -- which reweights the contribution of each class to the training loss -- was included as a fourth tuned hyperparameter (searched over 0.3--1.0, alongside \texttt{depth}, \texttt{learning\_rate}, and \texttt{l2\_leaf\_reg}; Section~\ref{sec:hpo}), so every outer fold learns the class weighting that is best for its own training data. The Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was deliberately not used: with only 57 Unconfirmed patients across 22 features, SMOTE's nearest-neighbour interpolation would synthesise clinically fabricated patients in a feature space that is already sparse, and, unless resampling were repeated correctly inside every one of the 400 selection folds and 40 optimization folds, risks leaking synthetic neighbours of test-fold patients into the corresponding training fold. Reweighting the loss via \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} avoids both failure modes: it never fabricates a patient, and it cannot leak across folds because it changes how existing training examples are weighted, not which examples exist. Keeping every training example a real, observed patient also matters downstream: the counterfactual permitted ranges (Section~\ref{sec:generalsetup}) are calculated from the observed distribution of each feature, so a model trained only on real patients keeps that distribution -- and the counterfactuals anchored to it -- clinically interpretable.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Optimization}
%==========================================
\label{sec:hpo}

CatBoost, selected on the full feature set as described in Section~\ref{sec:modelselection}, was carried forward to nested cross-validation hyperparameter optimization (Figure~\ref{fig:nested_cv}). An \textit{outer} \texttt{RepeatedStratifiedKFold} loop -- $k=4$ folds $\times$ 10 repeats, 40 outer iterations, \texttt{random\_state=42} -- provides the training/test partitions over which final performance is reported; nested inside every one of those 40 outer iterations, an \textit{inner} \texttt{StratifiedKFold} loop ($k=3$) partitions that iteration's outer-training set only, and is used exclusively for hyperparameter search. These two loops serve different purposes and operate on different data, which is the mechanism described in Section~\ref{sec:leakage}.

% Figure -- Nested cross-validation for hyperparameter optimization % =======================================
\begin{figure*}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{references/illustrations/nested_cv_optimization_compact.pdf}
    \caption{Nested cross-validation for CatBoost hyperparameter optimization. The outer \texttt{RepeatedStratifiedKFold} loop (4 folds $\times$ 10 repeats = 40 iterations) supplies the train/test partitions over which performance is ultimately reported; within each outer iteration, the inner \texttt{StratifiedKFold} loop ($k=3$) partitions the outer-training set only, and an Optuna TPE search of 100 trials tunes \texttt{depth}, \texttt{learning\_rate}, \texttt{l2\_leaf\_reg}, and \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} against the mean inner-validation AUPRC. The winning configuration is refit on the full outer-training set, an out-of-fold decision threshold is selected by maximising F1 on the inner out-of-fold precision--recall curve, and only then is the outer test fold touched, exactly once, to record the 13 metrics of Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}.     \label{fig:nested_cv}
\end{figure*}

Within each outer iteration, an Optuna Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler ran 100 trials, each fitting three CatBoost models -- one per inner fold -- and scoring the trial by the mean AUPRC across those three inner validation folds (\texttt{optimization\_metric: auprc}). Four hyperparameters were searched: tree \texttt{depth} (4--10, integer), \texttt{learning\_rate} (0.003--0.3, log-uniform), \texttt{l2\_leaf\_reg} (1.0--10.0, uniform), and \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} (0.3--1.0, uniform; Section~\ref{sec:classimbalance}). All other CatBoost settings were held constant across every trial, fold, and repeat: \texttt{loss\_function=Logloss}, \texttt{eval\_metric=AUC}, \texttt{iterations=500}, \texttt{early\_stopping\_rounds=50}.

After the search, the winning trial's parameters were refit once on the full outer-training set, and separately used to produce three further out-of-fold (OOF) refits over the same inner splits, yielding one held-out probability for every outer-training patient. A classification threshold was then selected, per outer iteration, as the value maximising the F1 score ($\beta=1$) on the precision--recall curve of those inner-OOF probabilities (\texttt{threshold\_selection\_metric: fscore}) -- never on the outer test fold. The refit model and its selected threshold were then applied once to the untouched outer test fold, recording the thirteen metrics in Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}: the threshold itself, accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1, F1.25, F1.5, F1.75, F2, the Youden index, ROC-AUC, and AUPRC. These per-outer-iteration values are pooled across all 40 outer iterations as a mean $\pm$ 95\% confidence interval (normal approximation, $z=1.96$, computed excluding any iteration where a metric is undefined for that fold), which is what is reported in Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats} in the Discussion.

\textit{One modeling pipeline, evaluated by cross-validation.} It bears stating explicitly, because it is easy to misread otherwise: the procedure above is \textit{one} modeling pipeline -- one algorithm (CatBoost), one hyperparameter-search procedure -- evaluated by 4-fold cross-validation repeated 10 times, not four, or forty, separate studies. Its primary performance claim is the single pooled mean $\pm$ 95\% CI over the 40 outer iterations described above. The models and splits of the \textit{first} of those 10 repeats are additionally persisted to disk (its four outer-fold refits, their training/test partitions, tuned hyperparameters, and thresholds) for reuse, without refitting, by the Explainability and Counterfactual Generation stages below. Consequently, when those later sections refer to ``the four models'' or ``model~0'' through ``model~3'', this denotes the four outer-fold refits of that single retained repeat of the one nested-CV procedure above -- not four independently designed experiments, and not a claim that any one of the four is individually authoritative. Their agreement or disagreement (e.g.\ in feature importance or SHAP ranking) is itself evidence about the stability of the one pipeline, not a comparison between competing pipelines.

\subsubsection{Explainability Analysis}
%======================================
\label{sec:explainability}

Post-hoc interpretability was assessed on the four persisted outer-fold models from the first cross-validation repeat. Each of the four is retrained from its stored outer-training partition using its own tuned hyperparameters (\texttt{best\_params}) from Section~\ref{sec:hpo} -- the same fold-specific configuration that Section~\ref{sec:hpo} selected, not a single shared configuration.

Two complementary views are reported. \textit{Per-fold} (``all splits'') diagnostics -- feature importance, SHAP value attributions, and the ROC/precision--recall curves -- are computed and plotted separately for each of the four fold models, to show whether the top predictors and their ranking are stable across folds rather than an artefact of one particular training partition. \textit{Pooled} diagnostics -- AUROC, AUPRC, calibration (reliability), and decision curve analysis -- are computed by concatenating the four fold models' predictions on their own respective test sets, giving one out-of-sample prediction per patient across the full 187-patient cohort, no patient scored by a model trained on them.

Because the four fold models come from independent per-fold hyperparameter searches (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}), each has its own predicted-probability scale (e.g.\ scale\_pos\_weight differs by fold), and pooling raw scores across those four scales does not behave like evaluating one fixed model. This has a specific, documented, and opposite-signed effect on the two families of pooled diagnostics, which the Discussion presents but does not re-derive: pooled AUROC and AUPRC are systematically \textit{more conservative} than the fold-wise mean, because ranking probabilities across incomparable scales degrades discrimination; pooled calibration is systematically \textit{more optimistic} than the fold-wise mean ECE, because folds that over-predict and folds that under-predict partially cancel once pooled. The pooled calibration curve therefore characterises a four-model mixture that is never deployed as such, and should be read alongside the fold-wise mean $\pm$ std rather than in place of it; Brier score is unaffected, being a per-observation average.

\subsection{Counterfactual Generation}
%*************************************
To provide interpretable insights into the predictions of the trained machine learning models, counterfactual explanations were generated using the DiCE (Diverse Counterfactual Explanations) framework \cite{mothilal2020explaining}. DiCE is well-suited for this study due to its model-agnostic capability, enabling its application across a wide range of classifiers, including complex ensemble models. Furthermore, it supports diversity-aware counterfactual generation, allowing the identification of multiple plausible feature modifications that can alter model predictions—an important requirement in clinical decision support. Its ability to incorporate feature constraints (e.g., permitted ranges and immutable variables) ensures that generated counterfactuals remain realistic and clinically meaningful.

\textit{Global Permitted Range}.
To ensure feasibility of generated counterfactuals, a global permitted range was defined for all continuous features, using every patient in the dataset (training and test partitions of the fold together, so the range describes the whole observed cohort rather than one fold's split of it) The bounds were computed as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \itemsep0em
    \item Minimum: $\max(0,\ x_{min} - \sigma)$
    \item Maximum: $x_{max} + \sigma$
\end{itemize}
where $x_{min}$ and $x_{max}$ denote the observed minimum and maximum values, respectively, of each feature over the whole cohort, and $\sigma$ is its standard deviation, also over the whole cohort. Bounding the minimum at 0 keeps a naturally non-negative clinical quantity (e.g.\ HbA1c) from being pushed below zero by the $-\sigma$ margin; the $+\sigma$ margin at both ends allows a counterfactual to propose a value a little beyond the observed range while remaining plausible.

\textit{DiCE Configuration Parameters}. The configuration of the DiCE framework is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dice_setup}. The genetic algorithm was selected as the optimization method because of the presence of multiple categorical features which renders the use of gradients for optimization not feasible. Genetic algorithm is also effectiveness in exploring high-dimensional feature spaces and generating diverse counterfactuals under multiple constraints.

\subsubsection{Local Counterfactual Analysis}
%============================================
We describe here how instances were chosen for counterfactual generation and how the permitted range was computed for each instance.

\textit{Targeted Instance Selection}.
Within each fold's test set, local counterfactual explanations were generated for misclassified and borderline cases. Borderline cases are instances whose predicted probability lies within $\delta = 0.08$ of \textit{that fold's own} tuned decision threshold (the \texttt{threshold\_delta} parameter) -- not a single threshold shared across folds. These instances represent cases where a small feature change may alter the predicted outcome, making them particularly informative for analysis; Figure~\ref{fig:univariable_discrimination}'s discrimination scale gives a sense of why so many patients fall this close to the boundary given the modest signal in most individual features.

\textit{Local Permitted Range}.
For each selected instance, an instance-specific permitted range was defined for each feature to vary. Similar to the global permitted range, this ensures that generated counterfactuals remain both realistic and tailored to the specific instance:

\begin{itemize}
    \itemsep0em
    \item Minimum: $\max(0,\ \mathrm{instance\_value} - \sigma)$
    \item Maximum: $\mathrm{instance\_value} + \sigma$
\end{itemize}
where, as for the global range, $\sigma$ is each feature's standard deviation over the whole cohort; the interval is centred on the patient's own observed value rather than the cohort mean, so a proposed change is scaled to a magnitude already seen in the cohort but anchored to where this particular patient starts from.

\subsubsection{Sufficiency and Necessity Analysis}
%=================================================
\label{sec:sufficiencynecessity}

Beyond the diverse counterfactual sets of Table~\ref{tab:local_cf}, two single-feature checks were run for each targeted instance to characterise the role of individual actionable features, rather than only sets of them. \textit{Sufficiency} asks whether varying one feature alone, with every other feature held fixed, is enough to flip the predicted class: DiCE is asked for one counterfactual (\texttt{total\_CFs=1}) with \texttt{features\_to\_vary} restricted to that single feature; if a valid counterfactual is returned within the iteration budget, that feature is sufficient on its own for that patient. \textit{Necessity} asks the complementary question -- whether a feature must change at all: DiCE is asked to vary every \textit{other} actionable feature, with the feature under test held fixed, across \texttt{nrepeats=5} random seeds and up to \texttt{total\_CFs=3} counterfactuals per seed; if no valid counterfactual is found across all 5 seeds, the held-fixed feature is deemed necessary, because the desired class could not be reached without it. Neither check is expected to succeed for every candidate instance -- the search is not guaranteed to converge within its iteration budget even when a valid counterfactual exists -- and the Discussion reports how many of the candidate instances yielded a usable result under this procedure.


\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Discussions should be brief and focused. 

In this section, we discuss the performance of the machine learning models evaluated for pre-diagnostic DPN screening, the rationale for selecting CatBoost over closely competing alternatives, and the hyperparameter optimization and local explainability of its predictions through feature importance and SHAP. We then turn to counterfactual explanation analysis --- the central contribution of this study --- describing the DiCE configuration used to generate patient-level counterfactuals, presenting detailed case studies, and synthesizing the aggregate feature-change patterns across all candidate instances. We close by outlining how this pipeline could be used by clinicians in practice, and by situating these results within a decision-support framing rather than as a deployment-ready diagnostic tool.

To be explicit about scope, this study is a proof-of-concept investigation into whether counterfactual explanations can make a machine-learning-based DPN screening model actionable for clinicians, not a validated or deployment-ready clinical instrument. To our knowledge, it is among the first studies to systematically integrate counterfactual explanations into a pre-diagnostic DPN screening pipeline intended for low-resource clinical settings where nerve conduction studies are costly or geographically inaccessible; the counterfactual analysis, rather than the underlying classifier's raw discriminative performance, is the primary contribution of this work. %% TODO-CITE: verify "among the first" claim against the literature review during the Claims Review pass.

\subsection{Feature Set and Model Selection}
%*******************************************

\subsubsection{Baseline Model Evaluation: Rationale and Metric Selection}
%========================================================================
The primary objective of this baseline evaluation was to identify the most promising classification algorithm for subsequent optimization as a machine learning-based pre-diagnostic screening tool for Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy (DPN). Fourteen algorithms spanning diverse learning paradigms, together with a naive majority-class baseline, were assessed using default hyperparameters and repeated stratified k-fold cross-validation (10 repeats × 4 folds; 40 total runs) to obtain stable, low-variance performance estimates without data leakage.
Given the inherent class imbalance in this dataset --- 130 DPN-confirmed patients versus 57 unconfirmed cases (69.5\%/30.5\%) --- AUPRC was chosen as the primary ranking metric in preference to ROC-AUC. This decision is well-supported in the methodological literature: ROC-AUC can present an overly optimistic picture of classifier performance under class imbalance because it incorporates the true negative rate, which inflates when the negative class is underrepresented. AUPRC, by contrast, focuses exclusively on the precision-recall trade-off and is therefore more sensitive to performance differences among classifiers on the minority class \cite{saito2015precision, davis2006relationship}. The naive baseline --- which predicts the majority class uniformly --- achieved an AUPRC of 0.695 (essentially the empirical prevalence) across all feature sets and conditions, despite attaining perfect sensitivity (1.0) and an accuracy of 69.5\%, illustrating precisely why accuracy and sensitivity alone are insufficient evaluation criteria in imbalanced screening tasks.

\subsubsection{Model Performance and Selection}
%==============================================
Across all 40 runs on the full feature set, ensemble tree-based methods consistently outperformed other model families (Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics}). Random Forest attained the highest mean AUPRC (0.892 $\pm$ 0.039), narrowly ahead of CatBoost (0.889 $\pm$ 0.044) and Extra Trees (0.885 $\pm$ 0.042); the three are statistically indistinguishable, with all three means falling well within one standard deviation of one another. Naive Bayes (0.884), Logistic Regression (0.880), RBF SVM (0.878), and LDA (0.878) followed closely behind, underscoring that the full feature set carries strong, model-agnostic signal for DPN status rather than favoring any single learning paradigm.

From a clinical deployment standpoint, the balance between sensitivity and specificity is of critical importance in pre-diagnostic screening. A tool intended to triage patients toward the more costly and less accessible Nerve Conduction Study (NCS) must prioritize minimizing false negatives (i.e., maintaining high sensitivity) to avoid denying care to patients who truly have DPN.

Under this clinical framing, Random Forest and CatBoost occupy essentially the same operating point: sensitivity of 0.916 and 0.913, and specificity of 0.529 and 0.512, respectively. RBF SVM achieved the highest sensitivity overall (0.938), but at the cost of the lowest specificity among the top models (0.453). Because Random Forest's raw AUPRC edge over CatBoost here is well within sampling noise, the choice between the two --- carried forward through the remainder of this pipeline --- cannot rest on this table alone; Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf} below makes the case for CatBoost on grounds that matter downstream rather than on this baseline ranking.

\subsubsection{Feature Set Analysis}
%===================================
The heatmap of average AUPRC across feature sets (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_heatmaps}) reveals several findings with direct clinical relevance. First, the top models (Random Forest, CatBoost, Extra Trees) are remarkably robust to the removal of most feature groups: ablating Clinical Profile (NoProf), Comorbidities (NoCom), or MNSI (NoMsi) produced negligible or no AUPRC change for these models (all within 0.01 of the full-feature-set value). This robustness implies that no single feature group is singularly indispensable, and that redundancy across the feature space is substantial. The exception is Neurological Examination features (NoNeuro), whose removal produced the largest drop observed for any ablation among the top models (Random Forest and CatBoost both fall to 0.86), suggesting these bedside signs are the least redundant predictors in the set.

The Sudoscan-only feature set (Sudo) understandably lowered performance for every model (Random Forest 0.82, CatBoost 0.83), while the additive Sudoscan-anchored feature sets (SudoProf, SudoCom, SudoNeuro, SudoMnsi) demonstrated modest but inconsistent improvements over Sudoscan-only, suggesting diminishing returns from adding other feature groups once Sudoscan data are available. One exception worth noting is that the SudoNeuro combination (Sudoscan with Neurological Examination findings) recovered to within 0.01 of full-feature-set performance for both top models (0.89 Random Forest, 0.90 CatBoost), indicating that simple bedside neurological signs --- vibration sensation, pinprick, light touch, and ankle reflex --- may complement electrochemical skin conductance data in a synergistic manner that benefits less flexible classifiers, and that a reduced Sudoscan+neuro-exam panel could be a viable lower-burden alternative to the full feature set in settings where Sudoscan is available but a complete profile, comorbidity, and MNSI workup is not practical.

We note that since these metrics are based on default hyperparameters, a more complete picture of feature importance will be explored during the hyperparameter optimization and explainability analysis that follow.

\subsubsection{Reproducibility and Stability of Estimates}
%=========================================================
The use of 10-repeat × 4-fold stratified cross-validation was chosen deliberately to obtain stable performance estimates given the modest sample size (n = 187). With a single k-fold split, estimates can exhibit high variance due to the stochastic partitioning of a small dataset \cite{kim2009estimating}.

Repeated cross-validation substantially reduces this variance, and the violin plot distributions (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_violins}) confirm that the top models maintain elevated, largely overlapping AUPRC distributions. Random Forest shows the highest median and a comparatively tight interquartile range; CatBoost's median and box are close behind, but with a longer lower tail reaching toward 0.75, indicating an occasional weaker fold rather than a systematically less stable ranking. This is a relevant nuance for the CatBoost-versus-Random-Forest discussion below: the two are close on central tendency but CatBoost is marginally more variable at this stage. The absence of hyperparameter tuning or threshold optimization in this phase is also intentional: these results represent genuine baseline performance, ensuring that the subsequent model selection and optimization pipeline is not confounded by information leakage from the full dataset.

\subsubsection{Study Limitations at the Baseline Evaluation Stage}
%=================================================================
Several limitations must be acknowledged at this stage. The dataset is relatively small (n = 187), and though repeated stratified cross-validation mitigates instability, external validation on an independent cohort remains necessary before clinical deployment. The class imbalance, while modest compared to many medical screening contexts, may still influence threshold-dependent metrics (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy) reported here, all of which used the default 0.5 probability threshold --- a suboptimal choice for screening applications. Threshold calibration and optimization are addressed in the subsequent modeling phase.

\subsubsection{Selected Feature Set and Model}
%=============================================
Despite Random Forest's narrow edge in raw AUPRC, CatBoost was selected as the model to carry forward into hyperparameter optimization and counterfactual generation, on the strength of a comparable sensitivity/specificity operating point at this stage combined with the methodological considerations detailed in Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf}. The full feature set was retained as the training data for all subsequent stages, since it consistently achieved high performance across multiple methods and the feature-set ablations discussed above showed no clinically compelling reason to discard any feature group at this point in the pipeline.

\subsubsection{CatBoost versus Random Forest: Methodological Justification}
%==========================================================================
\label{sec:catboost_vs_rf}
Because Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics} does not, by itself, favor CatBoost over Random Forest, the justification for carrying CatBoost --- rather than Random Forest --- through hyperparameter optimization, explainability analysis, and counterfactual generation rests on two considerations that this baseline table cannot capture.

First, small-sample regularization. With only 187 patients (approximately 140 per outer training fold), CatBoost's ordered boosting computes each tree's residuals using a permutation-restricted subset that excludes the point being scored, which is specifically designed to guard against the target leakage that plain gradient boosting is prone to when the same rows repeatedly inform both split statistics and gradients. Random Forest's bagged, full-depth CART trees are a different and less structurally constrained estimator at this sample size: individual trees can produce single- or few-sample leaves that bagging only partially averages away. CatBoost's symmetric (oblivious) tree structure, in which every node at a given depth shares the same split condition, further restricts the hypothesis space in a way that is particularly suited to small $n$. CatBoost's native handling of categorical comorbidity flags, and its \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} class reweighting in place of synthetic oversampling (Methods, Section~\ref{sec:leakage}), also avoid the one-hot dimensionality inflation and synthetic-patient fabrication risk that would otherwise compound on a cohort this small \cite{dorogush2018catboost}.

Second, and more decisively for this study's aims, compatibility with the counterfactual-generation pipeline. The DiCE genetic search used to produce every counterfactual in Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual} treats the model as a black box and drives its fitness function directly from \texttt{predict\_proba}. Random Forest's \texttt{predict\_proba} is a vote-share average assembled from leaf-class proportions across the ensemble; on a small, high-purity dataset, many leaves become pure or near-pure, producing flat regions of the probability surface with little gradient signal for a genetic search to climb. CatBoost's output, by contrast, is an additive sum of many shallow trees at a small learning rate passed through a sigmoid --- a smoother, higher-resolution score whose incremental changes translate into smoother, more monotonic probability movement. This property is exactly what the "minimal, sufficient change" logic behind a clinically usable counterfactual depends on, and it is the reason a coarser Random Forest probability surface would be expected to widen, not narrow, the flat regions where DiCE's genetic search already struggles (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility} below).

In short, the two models are statistically indistinguishable on this baseline table, so the deciding factors are properties that matter specifically for a 187-patient cohort and for a counterfactual-explanation pipeline built on \texttt{predict\_proba} --- not a leaderboard win for either model.

Before examining the four individually-tuned fold models used throughout the remainder of this study (Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}, next), we first establish CatBoost's overall performance across the pipeline's 4-fold structure at the level of the whole repeated cross-validation scheme: 10 independent repetitions of the 4-fold split, yielding 40 total evaluation runs, with aggregate estimates presented in Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}. This overall view is the appropriate starting point precisely because any single partitioning of a modest-sized dataset carries some risk of being unrepresentative of the underlying data distribution \cite{kim2009estimating}; establishing that the pipeline's performance is stable across 40 independent partitions, before selecting any one specific set of fold models to analyze in detail, avoids drawing conclusions from what could otherwise be a fold-specific artifact. As with the selection stage (Section~\ref{sec:models}), these 40 runs represent one modeling pipeline evaluated repeatedly, not 40 separately trained studies.

The mean AUPRC across all 40 runs was 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.871--0.902, STD = 0.049), with a confidence interval spanning just 3.1 percentage points, indicating that model performance is highly reproducible across varying data splits, an important criterion for clinical tool reliability \cite{steyerberg2010assessing}.

The mean sensitivity of 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.861--0.912) --- the most clinically critical metric for a pre-diagnostic screening instrument --- remained high and reasonably well-constrained, with an 95\% CI spanning roughly 5 percentage points and a STD of 0.083. This indicates that the model's ability to correctly identify DPN-positive patients is comparatively robust to partition variability, a finding of direct clinical relevance: in a screening context where the consequence of a missed DPN case includes delayed intervention, progressive nerve damage, and increased risk of foot ulceration or lower-limb amputation, a consistently high sensitivity is more valuable than a high average sensitivity with wide variability \cite{tesfaye2010}. By contrast, specificity showed the greatest instability across runs (mean = 0.527, STD = 0.141, 95\% CI: 0.483--0.571), confirming that the model's ability to correctly exclude DPN-negative patients was more sensitive to partition composition. This reflects the relatively smaller size of the Unconfirmed-DPN class (n = 57) and, as the per-fold analysis below shows, fold-by-fold variability in both Bayesian threshold selection and tuned hyperparameters.

The mean F1-score of 0.845 (95\% CI: 0.831--0.860) summarizes the balance between precision and sensitivity in a single number, and its reasonably narrow confidence interval indicates this balance is stable across partitions. Because F1 weights precision and sensitivity equally, it should be read alongside sensitivity and specificity above rather than in isolation: for this screening use case, where minimizing false negatives is the clinical priority, sensitivity remains the more directly relevant single metric, with F1 serving as a complementary summary of overall precision-recall balance.

The mean Youden Index of 0.413 (95\% CI: 0.370--0.456, STD = 0.138) was the most variable metric in relative terms, consistent with its dependence on both sensitivity and specificity, the latter being inherently unstable in imbalanced settings. Nevertheless, a Youden Index above 0.37 across the full confidence interval confirms that the model operates meaningfully above chance discrimination at the selected thresholds. The relatively wide CI of the Youden Index further underscores that composite balance metrics are more partition-sensitive than sensitivity-dominant metrics in this class distribution, reinforcing the rationale for prioritizing AUPRC and sensitivity as primary evaluation criteria for this use case.

Taken together, these repeated cross-validation results establish that CatBoost's performance across the pipeline's 4-fold structure is statistically stable and not the product of favorable data splitting. This reproducibility is a prerequisite for treating the model as a credible decision-support component; it is not, on its own, evidence of readiness for clinical deployment, which would additionally require external validation (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). With this overall picture established, we next turn to the specific four fold models selected to represent this pipeline throughout the remainder of this Discussion.

\subsubsection{Model Selection, Performance, and Clinical Suitability for Pre-Diagnostic Screening}
%==================================================================================================
The repeated cross-validation above characterizes CatBoost's performance in aggregate, but the Explainability and Counterfactual Generation analyses that follow require concrete, trained models rather than an abstract average across 40 partitions: SHAP attributions and DiCE counterfactuals are computed against one specific fitted CatBoost ensemble per fold, and no meaningful "average model" can be constructed by averaging 40 independently grown tree ensembles with 40 different tuned hyperparameter sets. Some single repeat's four outer-fold models must therefore be selected and persisted for reuse, without refitting, by every subsequent stage of this Discussion --- and, as reported below, all downstream analysis of feature importance, SHAP explanations, and counterfactual generation is dependent on whichever repeat is chosen for this purpose.

We use the first of the ten repeats (fixed by \texttt{random\_state=42}, Section~\ref{sec:hpo}) as this representative set of four fold models. Two considerations defend this choice. First, it is fixed by the pipeline's design rather than selected after comparing repeats' outcomes, so it cannot reflect cherry-picking a favorably-performing repeat; the same repeat would have been used regardless of how it happened to score. Second, its representativeness is directly supported by the concordance with the pooled 40-repeat estimates above: as the discussion below shows, the first repeat's per-fold mean estimates (Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}) fall within roughly one standard deviation of the full 40-repeat pooled estimates (Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}) for every metric, indicating that this particular repeat is not an atypical draw from the pipeline's overall performance distribution. This second point should nonetheless be read as a plausibility check rather than a guarantee: it confirms the first repeat's \emph{aggregate} behavior is unremarkable, not that every individual fold within it is equally representative --- Model 0's comparatively low sensitivity, discussed below, is one place where this repeat does show fold-specific behavior worth keeping in mind when interpreting the Explainability and Counterfactual results that build on it.

Table \ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat} shows the best parameters and performance metrics for the four models, together with their mean and standard deviation. Mean specificity (0.561) and F1 (0.842) are modest improvements over the baseline unoptimized CatBoost model (0.512 and 0.858, see Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics}), while mean sensitivity (0.870) is slightly below the unoptimized baseline (0.913) --- a direct consequence of per-fold Bayesian threshold optimization trading some sensitivity for a better-balanced operating point rather than maximizing recall alone. For a pre-diagnostic screening instrument intended to triage patients toward the more resource-intensive and often geographically inaccessible Nerve Conduction Study (NCS), the clinical priority remains high sensitivity, so this trade-off is examined further at the pipeline level in the repeated cross-validation analysis above.

Per-fold thresholds were tightly clustered (0.483--0.500, a range of just 0.017), with each fold's Bayesian search converging on a similar operating point near 0.49, so this narrow range cannot explain the considerable spread in mean specificity (0.561, range: 0.357--0.643). That spread more plausibly reflects two compounding sources of per-fold variability instead: which Unconfirmed-DPN patients happened to fall into each fold's held-out test set, and the substantially different hyperparameters each fold's independent search converged on --- most notably \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}, which ranged from 0.633 (Model 3) to 0.954 (Model 2) and directly governs how the model trades sensitivity against specificity (Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}).

Variability in Youden Index across folds (0.297 to 0.549) highlights that Model 3 achieved the most balanced sensitivity-specificity operating point among the four, potentially attributable to the particular composition of its training partition. Nevertheless, sensitivity remained above 0.90 for three of the four folds (Model 1: 0.939, Models 2 and 3: 0.906), with Model 0 the outlier at 0.727 --- a fold-specific weak point that the repeated cross-validation analysis above and the ensemble-level counterfactual discussion (Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual}) both help contextualize. The use of 4-fold stratified cross-validation --- rather than a single train-test split --- ensured that every patient contributed to model evaluation, reducing selection bias and providing a more reliable estimate of out-of-sample performance for a dataset of this size \cite{steyerberg2010assessing}.

The F1-scores, ranging from 0.774 to 0.879 (mean: 0.842), and AUPRC values ranging from 0.837 to 0.941 (mean: 0.893), indicate that all four fold models substantially exceed the no-skill baseline of 0.695 (Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics}), with CatBoost and gradient-boosting approaches generally recognized for handling mixed feature types and class imbalance without extensive preprocessing --- properties especially beneficial in clinical tabular data \cite{dorogush2018catboost}.

Figure \ref{fig:catboost_auprc} shows the pooled Precision-Recall curve built from the held-out predictions of all four CatBoost fold models. The pooled AUPRC is 0.858 (95\% CI: 0.795--0.916), and the mean $\pm$ std of the four individual per-fold AUPRC values is 0.893 $\pm$ 0.043 (Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}); both are consistent with the 40-repeat pooled mean of 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.871--0.902, Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}) established above, reinforcing that this first-repeat AUPRC estimate is representative rather than an artifact of this specific repeat. As discussed in the figure caption, the pooled and mean-of-folds estimates here are two distinct but complementary summary statistics from the same four fold models, not conflicting estimates. Given the inherent class imbalance in the study cohort --- where 69.5\% of patients (130/187) carried confirmed DPN --- the AUPRC is the more informative summary metric, as it is sensitive to model behavior among the minority (Unconfirmed DPN) class and is not inflated by the abundance of true negatives \cite{saito2015precision, davis2006relationship}.

Across all four folds, CatBoost feature importances (Figure~\ref{fig:feature_importances}) and SHAP-based explanations (Figure~\ref{fig:shap}) converged on a consistent set of clinically meaningful predictors, reinforcing the model's interpretability and lending biological plausibility to its decision logic --- a critical consideration for clinical acceptance of machine learning tools \cite{rudin2019}.

Three predictors were consistently top-ranked across all four folds: decreased ankle reflex (DEC\_AR; importance 8.35--13.82, SHAP 0.047--0.079), diabetes mellitus duration (DM\_DUR; importance 7.71--11.62, SHAP 0.017--0.059), and feet Sudoscan asymmetry (FEET\_PCT\_ASYM; importance 7.02--11.74, SHAP 0.010--0.048). Decreased vibration sensation (DEC\_VS; importance 4.64--9.81, SHAP 0.019--0.068) and the MNSI score (importance 5.35--10.55, SHAP 0.013--0.047) followed closely, with MNSI notably reaching the second-highest importance of any feature in Model 1 (10.55, just behind DEC\_AR's 9.87 in that fold). This is clinically plausible: loss of ankle deep tendon reflex and impaired vibration perception are among the earliest and most reliable large-fiber neurological signs of DPN, and long-standing hyperglycemia (indexed here by diabetes duration) is a well-established driver of cumulative nerve damage \cite{tesfaye2010}. Their consistent dominance across all four independently trained folds indicates that the model is anchoring its discriminative reasoning on diagnostically recognizable clinical signs rather than idiosyncratic artifacts of any single training partition.

Beyond this top group, the Sudoscan-derived cluster contributed substantial predictive weight in aggregate: the neuropathy score (NS; importance 6.68--7.88, SHAP 0.021--0.042), hand Sudoscan asymmetry (HAND\_PCT\_ASYM; importance 5.27--7.52, SHAP 0.008--0.032), and the cardiac autonomic score (CAS; importance 5.52--6.84, SHAP 0.014--0.017) each ranked in the upper half of the feature set in every fold, with foot- and hand-level mean electrochemical skin conductance (FEET\_MEAN\_ESC, HAND\_MEAN\_ESC) contributing more moderately. Sudoscan measures small-fiber sudomotor function through galvanic skin response and has been used as a rapid, noninvasive surrogate marker of sudomotor neuropathy, an early manifestation of diabetic small-fiber involvement that frequently precedes or accompanies the large-fiber involvement detectable by NCS \cite{casellini2013sudoscan}. The co-prominence of large-fiber neurological examination findings, diabetes duration, and multiple Sudoscan-derived small-fiber parameters across all folds suggests the model is integrating complementary information spanning both large- and small-fiber neuropathy pathways, rather than relying on a single modality --- consistent with the finding at the unoptimized-baseline stage that the SudoNeuro feature combination alone recovered nearly full-feature-set performance (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_heatmaps}).

HbA1c (HBA1C; importance 5.21--8.13, SHAP 0.007--0.025), age (AGE; importance 5.51--6.48, SHAP 0.014--0.028), and insulin use (INSULIN; importance 1.08--2.58, SHAP 0.004--0.009) contributed consistently but at lower magnitudes than the top predictors above. This is a point of direct relevance to the counterfactual analysis that follows (Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual}): HbA1c and insulin use are comparatively modest drivers of the model's overall feature importance, yet --- because they are the features most amenable to clinical intervention --- they dominate the counterfactual "recipes" DiCE proposes for reclassification. Feature importance and counterfactual salience answer different questions (what the model relies on across the whole cohort, versus what can be changed for a given patient) and are not expected to rank features identically.

In contrast, the comorbidities hypertension (HPN) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) received consistently low feature importance and SHAP values across all four folds, while peripheral arterial occlusive disease (PAOD) and Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) received near-zero importance in every fold, suggesting these comorbidities do not independently contribute much to DPN prediction in this cohort --- a finding that likely reflects their comparatively low prevalence in the study population and their pathophysiological distinction from diabetic neuropathy itself.

\paragraph{Feature Actionability and the Counterfactual Candidate Pool}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
A prerequisite for clinically meaningful counterfactuals is that the features being altered must correspond to interventions that are realistically achievable. Accordingly, features were stratified by actionability prior to counterfactual generation (Table~\ref{tab:cf_features}). Immutable demographic attributes (sex, age, DM duration) and examination-derived measurements (neurological findings, MNSI, Sudoscan readings) were excluded, as these do not correspond to modifiable patient states. This exclusion is not merely a technical constraint: counterfactuals generated from non-actionable features risk producing explanations that are statistically valid but clinically misleading \cite{karimi2020}. The final actionable feature set --- HbA1c, insulin use, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CKD, and PAOD --- reflects a pragmatic and evidence-aligned subset of DPN risk factors for which clinical pathways exist. As the case studies below illustrate, however, being on this actionable list is necessary but not sufficient for a specific counterfactual to be a realistic clinical recommendation: DiCE is free to propose \emph{introducing} a comorbidity such as PAOD or CKD (an actionable feature can move in either direction) even though inducing a new disease is never a real intervention, so each proposed change still requires clinical judgment about its direction, not just its feature identity.

\paragraph{Candidate Instances and Counterfactual Generation}
%------------------------------------------------------------
\label{sec:cf-plausibility}
We identified misclassified instances (incorrect predictions) and borderline instances (prediction probability within 0.08 of the fold's own decision threshold) as candidate instances for counterfactual generation. A total of 61 candidates were identified across the four folds (42 misclassified, 19 borderline; Table~\ref{tab:localcf-model-level}), but only 8 yielded a usable counterfactual within the 1-hour per-patient generation budget --- none of them from Model 2, whose 12 candidates all failed to converge. Table~\ref{tab:localcf-model-level} provides a summary of the counterfactuals generated per model while Table~\ref{tab:localcf-listing} provides details of the 8 candidates that successfully generated counterfactuals.

This low yield is a direct, documented consequence of how DiCE's genetic search behaves on this dataset, not an arbitrary generation failure, and it is worth being explicit about the configuration and its limits so that the counterfactuals reported below are read with the right degree of confidence. Counterfactuals were generated with DiCE's genetic algorithm (\texttt{DiverseCF}), targeting up to 20 diverse counterfactuals per patient over 3 repeated search attempts, with proximity weight 0.5, diversity weight 1.0, a categorical penalty of 0.1, and posthoc sparsity pruning (binary search, parameter 0.05) applied for maximally sparse, clinically legible output. Separately from the yield problem, the genetic search on this dataset and schema has an independently documented tendency to occasionally violate the \texttt{features\_to\_vary} constraint --- i.e., to propose changing a feature explicitly marked immutable, such as SUBJ or a Sudoscan reading --- in a small fraction of counterfactuals for a given patient, regardless of model or configuration; every reported counterfactual in this study has been screened against this constraint and any violating rows removed before reporting, and the removed rows are retained separately (as \texttt{\_unactionable.csv} companions to two of the three case studies below) as a transparency record rather than silently discarded. Both behaviors --- the outright non-convergence for an entire fold, and the intermittent constraint violations within an otherwise successful run --- stem from the same underlying cause: a genetic search's fitness landscape can be poorly conditioned in some regions of a small, high-dimensional clinical feature space, and CatBoost's smoother \texttt{predict\_proba} surface (Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf}) mitigates, but does not eliminate, this fragility. In practice, this means the 8 patients discussed below are not a representative sample of "typical" DPN cases; they are the subset for which the search happened to succeed, and the absence of a counterfactual for any other candidate is evidence of search difficulty, not evidence that no clinically meaningful counterfactual exists for that patient.

\paragraph{Patient 20 --- Borderline False Positive (Model 3)}
%-------------------------------------------------------------
\textit{Patient Profile}: Female, 51 years old; diabetes duration 1 year; HbA1c 10.3\%; on insulin; no hypertension or PAOD; dyslipidemia present; no CKD. Predicted as Confirmed DPN at a probability of 0.498, a hair above Model 3's threshold of 0.492 (margin = 0.006) --- essentially a coin flip --- despite an unconfirmed clinical diagnosis.

With a margin of only 0.006, Patient 20 sits closer to her fold's decision boundary than any other candidate in this study, and the 23 counterfactuals generated for her reflect that: this is the most parsimonious case in the dataset, with a mean sparsity of 1.74 features (Table~\ref{tab:localcf-listing}). Several counterfactuals require changing only a single actionable feature: stopping insulin alone (INSULIN: 1$\to$0), managing dyslipidemia alone (DSLPDMIA: 1$\to$0), or reducing HbA1c alone by as little as 2.3--2.7 points. Two-feature combinations are similarly small --- for example, stopping insulin together with a 0.2--0.3 point HbA1c reduction. Across all 23 counterfactuals, HbA1c is the most frequently changed feature (16 of 23), followed by insulin (11 of 23) and dyslipidemia (7 of 23); hypertension, PAOD, and CKD changes are rare (2, 1, and 3 instances respectively). One counterfactual moves the immutable feature DM\_DUR alongside HbA1c and was filtered out as unactionable (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}) rather than reported as a valid recommendation. Because the margin is so small and the counterfactuals so sparse, this case illustrates the scenario where counterfactual explanation is most directly useful: a patient one small, plausible change away from a different classification, for whom the clinical recommendation --- continued glycemic management, with insulin and dyslipidemia review as secondary levers --- is both actionable and consistent with standard diabetes care.

\paragraph{Patient 40 --- Borderline True Positive (Model 0)}
%------------------------------------------------------------
\textit{Patient Profile}: Female, 62 years old; diabetes duration 5 years; HbA1c 7.5\%; on insulin; hypertension, PAOD, and dyslipidemia present; no CKD. Correctly predicted as Confirmed DPN at a probability of 0.513, just above Model 0's threshold of 0.499 (margin = 0.014), matching the confirmed clinical diagnosis.

Patient 40 is a correctly classified borderline case, and the 25 generated counterfactuals show what it would take to move her below the threshold. HbA1c is changed in every single one of the 25 counterfactuals, the only patient in this study for which that is true, and the single most parsimonious counterfactual overall is a 0.5-point HbA1c reduction (7.5\% $\to$ 7.0\%) with no other change --- the smallest single-feature perturbation observed for any patient (L1 = 0.5). Two-feature combinations largely pair a similar HbA1c reduction with stopping insulin (18 of 25 counterfactuals touch insulin) or resolving PAOD (6 of 25); hypertension and CKD are altered less often (4 and 2 instances). Two counterfactuals instead moved the immutable SUBJ feature alongside a larger HbA1c reduction and were filtered out as unactionable. Because this patient is both correctly classified and borderline, the clinical reading is different from a misclassification: the counterfactual does not correct an error, it quantifies how close this patient sits to being reclassified, supporting a watchful-waiting recommendation centered on glycemic control rather than an urgent referral.

\paragraph{Patient 123 --- Confident False Negative (Model 1)}
%-------------------------------------------------------------
\textit{Patient Profile}: Female, 68 years old; diabetes duration 3 years; HbA1c 7.18\%; not on insulin; hypertension present; no PAOD, dyslipidemia, or CKD. Incorrectly predicted as Unconfirmed DPN at a probability of 0.269, well below Model 1's threshold of 0.5 (margin = 0.231), despite a confirmed clinical diagnosis.

Patient 123 is the only false negative among the eight successful instances and, at a margin of 0.231, the least borderline case in this study --- Model 1 places her confidently in the wrong class. The 25 counterfactuals generated for her therefore describe what would need to be different for the model to correctly flag her as Confirmed DPN, and every one of them changes HbA1c downward (typically by 1.2--2.2 points, e.g. 7.18\% $\to$ 5.0\%), the largest and most consistent single driver of any patient in this study; hypertension is the second most frequent change (removed in 15 of 25), followed by PAOD (introduced in 11 of 25), insulin (started in 7 of 25), dyslipidemia (introduced in 2 of 25), and CKD (introduced in 1 of 25). This is a case where the actionable/non-actionable distinction matters most: reducing HbA1c and resolving hypertension are legitimate clinical targets, and the smallest counterfactual (HbA1c $-$1.78 combined with resolving hypertension) reflects exactly that; but introducing PAOD, dyslipidemia, or CKD --- which several other counterfactuals propose --- is never a real intervention, only a marker that the model associates the presence of those comorbidities with a higher DPN probability in this training partition. No separate unactionable-CF file was generated for this patient, because every altered feature here happens to fall on the actionable list (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}); the actionable/non-actionable distinction still has to be applied at the level of the specific proposed change (removing a condition versus introducing one), not merely at the level of which feature was touched. Clinically, this is the case that most directly illustrates the stakes of a false negative in this screening framework: a patient with confirmed DPN, comfortably below the referral threshold, for whom the counterfactual identifies glycemic control and hypertension management as the levers most associated --- in this model's training data --- with the confirmed-DPN phenotype she actually has.

\paragraph{Synthesis of the Three Case Studies}
%----------------------------------------------
\label{sec:cf-synthesis}
Read together, these three cases span the practically relevant range of what a counterfactual can say to a clinician. For Patient 20 and Patient 40, both borderline (margins of 0.006 and 0.014), the counterfactuals are small, single- or two-feature perturbations dominated by HbA1c --- exactly what "minimal sufficient change" should look like in a case that is genuinely close to the threshold, and directly actionable as a glycemic-management recommendation. For Patient 123, confidently misclassified (margin 0.231), the counterfactual set is necessarily larger and more mixed, some of it non-actionable (introducing a comorbidity is not an intervention), but the actionable core --- HbA1c reduction and hypertension management --- still identifies the specific risk factors that, according to the model's training partition, separate her from the confirmed-DPN patients it recognizes. Across all three cases, HbA1c is the dominant and most consistently plausible lever, echoing its central role in the aggregate-level analysis below (Section~\ref{sec:aggregate-cf}); insulin and, in Patient 123's case, hypertension are the next most useful actionable levers, while comorbidity-introduction counterfactuals are the clearest recurring example of a technically valid but clinically meaningless suggestion that a human reader must filter out. This is precisely the caveat raised in Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}: a generated counterfactual is a candidate explanation to be clinically reviewed, not an automatic prescription.

HbA1c modification remains the single most frequently proposed change across the aggregate counterfactual set (144 of 166, 86.7\%), but the pattern is now more genuinely multifactorial than the previous run of this pipeline suggested: insulin (81, 48.8\%) and dyslipidemia (59, 35.5\%) are substantial secondary levers, not minor contributors. This is clinically coherent and well-supported in the literature: chronic hyperglycemia --- indexed by HbA1c --- drives the oxidative and metabolic mechanisms underlying peripheral nerve damage in diabetic patients, and tighter glycemic control has been demonstrated to reduce DPN incidence and progression \cite{callaghan2012,tesfaye2010}. The counterfactual analysis, thus, not only identifies HbA1c as the most frequently implicated actionable feature, but operationalizes this importance as a concrete clinical recommendation: patients flagged as high-risk by the model may be redirected toward glycemic optimization, insulin therapy review, and dyslipidemia management, in roughly that order of frequency, before or alongside a Nerve Conduction Study referral.

Model-level heterogeneity (Figure~\ref{fig:globalcf}) reinforces a point already made about the selection and optimization stages above: each fold is a different sample of the same 187-patient cohort, and its counterfactual "signature" reflects the specific patients who happened to be borderline or misclassified in that fold, not four independent findings to be reconciled. Model 1's hypertension/PAOD-led pattern, for instance, is a description of Patient 123 specifically (Section~\ref{sec:instance-level-cf}), not evidence that hypertension generally outranks HbA1c in this cohort.

Despite HbA1c's dominant role, the mean sparsity values for the three models with successful counterfactuals ranged from 2.05 to 2.52 (Table~\ref{tab:localcf-model-level}), indicating that most counterfactual reversals require changing only two to three of the six available actionable features, not the majority of them. Combined with the feature-frequency pattern above, this suggests the models rely on a small number of co-occurring risk factors --- most often HbA1c together with one of insulin, dyslipidemia, or hypertension --- rather than a single feature threshold or the full comorbidity profile, a pattern consistent with the known multifactorial pathogenesis of DPN \cite{feldman2019}. Dyslipidemia and hypertension's substantial representation (59 and 49 instances respectively, Table~\ref{tab:localcf-changed}) is consistent with their established roles as peripheral neuropathy risk amplifiers through vascular and metabolic mechanisms \cite{jaiswal2017,callaghan2012}.

In terms of distance from the reference instance, Model 1 (represented only by Patient 123) has the highest mean L1/L2 distance among the three successful models (3.27/2.20), consistent with that patient's large decision margin (0.231) requiring a larger perturbation to cross; Model 3 produced the most parsimonious counterfactuals overall (sparsity mean 2.05, L1 mean 2.80), consistent with Patients 20 and 172 both being close to their threshold.

The integration of counterfactual analysis into this pre-diagnostic screening framework addresses a critical barrier to ML adoption in clinical settings: interpretability. Black-box predictions, however accurate, are unlikely to be acted upon by clinicians without a justification that aligns with their clinical reasoning \cite{rudin2019}. By grounding model explanations in actionable, modifiable features and framing them as "what-if" scenarios consistent with clinical intervention pathways, the counterfactual approach offers a practical bridge between statistical prediction and clinical decision-making --- provided, as emphasized throughout this section, that each proposed change is still read by a clinician rather than acted on automatically. Notably, the approach does not require clinicians to understand the internal mechanics of the CatBoost model; instead, it presents outputs in a form familiar to clinical reasoning: "If this patient's HbA1c and insulin regimen were better controlled, the model would not flag them for NCS referral."

This has implications not only for individual patient management but for population-level screening program design, as the most frequently changed features across all counterfactuals --- HbA1c, insulin use, and dyslipidemia --- directly correspond to the targets of existing diabetes complication management guidelines \cite{ada2024}.

\subsection{A Proposed Clinical Usage Workflow}
%**********************************************
The case studies and aggregate patterns above suggest a concrete, low-burden way this pipeline could fit into an existing diabetes clinic workflow, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:clinician_workflow}. At a routine follow-up visit, the clinic already collects (or can readily collect) the profile, comorbidity, MNSI, and brief neurological examination data this model uses, optionally supplemented by Sudoscan; no Nerve Conduction Study is required to generate a risk score. The CatBoost model then produces a predicted probability of Confirmed DPN against its fold-calibrated threshold. For patients whose prediction is comfortably below threshold, no further action is needed beyond routine follow-up. For patients who are flagged or borderline, the corresponding DiCE counterfactual is reviewed alongside the prediction: it identifies the smallest realistic change, among the six actionable features, that would move the patient across the threshold. As Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility} and the case studies in Section~\ref{sec:instance-level-cf} make clear, this review step requires a clinician to filter the suggestion --- discarding any proposal that amounts to introducing a new comorbidity, and evaluating the remainder (typically glycemic control, insulin initiation, or management of hypertension/dyslipidemia) against the patient's broader clinical picture. The output of that review then supports one of two actions: patients with a high-confidence flag and few or no actionable counterfactual pathways are prioritized for Nerve Conduction Study referral, while patients with a borderline flag and a sparse, actionable counterfactual are directed toward targeted risk-factor management and re-screened at a subsequent visit.

We emphasize, as throughout this Discussion, that this workflow is a proposal for how the pipeline's outputs could support clinical decision-making, not a validated clinical protocol: the risk score and the counterfactual are decision-support inputs, and the referral or treatment decision remains the clinician's.

    \caption{Proposed clinician workflow combining the CatBoost risk score with DiCE counterfactual review. Steps 1--2 require only routinely collected clinical data and no Nerve Conduction Study. Step 3 (outlined in purple) is reviewed only for flagged or borderline patients and requires clinician judgment to separate actionable suggestions (e.g., glycemic control) from suggestions that are not real interventions (e.g., introducing a comorbidity). \subsection{Limitations}
%***********************
\label{sec:limitations}
Beyond the stage-specific limitations already noted (Section~\ref{sec:models}: baseline evaluation; repeated cross-validation above: model stability), several limitations apply to the study as a whole and should temper how these results are read.

The data come from a single clinic and a single patient cohort (n = 187). Even with repeated stratified cross-validation, this cannot substitute for external validation on an independent cohort, and the reported performance and counterfactual patterns may not generalize to populations with different demographics, comorbidity prevalence, or diagnostic practices. Relatedly, the cohort was not randomly sampled from a defined population, so some degree of selection bias --- for instance, toward patients already referred for the neurological work-up that established their DPN status --- cannot be ruled out.

This study is a proof-of-concept decision-support investigation, not a validated or deployment-ready clinical tool. No prospective testing has been performed: the model and its counterfactuals have not been evaluated in a live clinical workflow, and it is not known whether clinicians presented with these outputs would make different or better referral decisions than they would otherwise. Deploying a screening model of this kind carries real risks if these steps are skipped --- a false negative could delay a diagnosis, and a false positive could prompt an unnecessary referral or, more subtly, undue reassurance to a clinician who over-trusts a favorable counterfactual --- which is precisely why we frame this work as decision support requiring clinician oversight at every step (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}), rather than as an autonomous diagnostic tool.

Finally, the small dataset size, while addressed methodologically through repeated cross-validation and CatBoost's regularization properties (Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf}), remains a fundamental constraint: it limits the granularity of subgroup analysis, contributes to the specificity instability documented above, and is the same underlying reason a subset of counterfactual candidates could not be resolved by the DiCE genetic search (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}).
