\abstract{\textbf{Background:} Diabetic peripheral neuropathy is the most common polyneuropathy among Filipinos with diabetes but goes undiagnosed in up to 81\% of cases, as confirmation requires a costly and often geographically inaccessible nerve conduction study. We asked whether a model using only data a diabetes clinic can collect without electrodiagnostic equipment can prioritize patients for that referral, and whether counterfactual explanations can turn its predictions into actionable guidance.

\textbf{Methods:} Of 190 adults with diabetes recruited at a tertiary hospital in Quezon City, Philippines (2022--2023), 187 with complete data were labeled Confirmed (130) or Unconfirmed (57) neuropathy under Toronto consensus criteria; the nerve conduction study defining that label was excluded from every predictor set. Fourteen algorithms and twelve feature subsets, spanning clinical profile, comorbidities, the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument, bedside neurological examination, and Sudoscan, were benchmarked by ten repeats of stratified four-fold cross-validation on the area under the precision-recall curve. The selected model was tuned by nested Bayesian optimization, then interpreted through feature importance, Shapley attributions, and counterfactuals restricted to six clinically actionable features.

\textbf{Results:} Ensemble tree methods clustered indistinguishably at the top (best 0.892 versus a no-skill baseline of 0.695); CatBoost was carried forward for its fit to a small cohort and its smoother probability surface for counterfactual search. \section{Background}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{introduction}

% The Introduction section expands on the background of the work (some overlap with the Abstract is acceptable). The introduction should not include subheadings.


Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is the most common type of polyneuropathy affecting 42-58\% of Filipinos \cite{ropper2019adams, araneta2006hyperglycemia, dagang2016validation}.
Characterized by progressive nerve damage, DPN often presents with symptoms such as pain, numbness, and loss of sensation in the extremities, particularly the feet. Beyond its direct impact on quality of life, DPN is a major risk factor for foot ulcers, infections, and lower-limb amputations, contributing significantly to healthcare burden.

Despite being associated with reduced quality of life and debilitating complications,
DPN is underdiagnosed in up to 81\% of patients \cite{vinik2012neuropathy, ziegler2018painful}. Hurdles in the early diagnosis of DPN includes the insidious progression, inconsistent disease presentation and the need for complicated equipment for definitive diagnosis \cite{vinik2012neuropathy, ziegler2018painful, casellini2013sudoscan}.

Skin biopsy with quantitation of intraepidermal nerve fiber density is still the gold standard in detecting small fiber neuropathy, while sudorimetry is a rapidly evolving technology that can easily be integrated into clinical practice. The development of such technologies like the Sudoscan allows noninvasive and easy-to-perform tests for detection of small fiber neuropathy based on reverse iontophoresis and chronoamperometry to measure sudomotor function \cite{mayaudon:2010newsimple, vinik2015newage}.

Early detection and management of DPN are therefore critical, yet remain challenging due to the multifactorial nature of the disease and the heterogeneity of patient profiles. In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool for modeling complex, nonlinear relationships in medical data. By leveraging large clinical datasets, ML algorithms can identify patterns and interactions among risk factors that may not be readily apparent through traditional statistical approaches. 

Numerous studies have developed ML models for DPN with good discriminatory performance (AUCs ranging from 0.64 to 0.93), employing algorithms such as random forest, XGBoost, and gradient boosting over clinical, biochemical, and other feature sets \cite{ma2025machine,jiang2024multifeature,cui2023study,lian2023Study,Wei2024construction}. Predictive performance alone, however, has not been enough to secure clinical adoption: ensemble and deep learning methods are routinely criticized as ``black boxes,'' and the field of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has grown in response. Feature importance scores, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations), LIME, and Anchor explanations are now commonly reported alongside DPN and other clinical prediction models, and have been combined with multimodal data and human-in-the-loop feedback to identify key risk factors \cite{wu2025prognostic,jiang2024multifeature,pandey2024decoding,wu2024interpretable,sheikh2025predicting,nguyen2024interpretable,alHudaibi2025explainable,lalwani2025generative}. These approaches describe which features a model relies on, but they describe associations rather than actions, which is what clinical decision-making requires.

Counterfactual explanations represent a more recent and clinically meaningful approach within XAI. Instead of merely explaining why a prediction was made, counterfactuals answer ``what-if'' questions by identifying minimal changes to a patient's features that would lead to a different predicted outcome. For example, a counterfactual explanation might suggest how modifying certain clinical variables---such as glycemic control or body mass index---could reduce a patient's predicted risk of developing DPN. This aligns closely with the needs of personalized medicine, where interventions are tailored to individual patients based on modifiable risk factors. Several methodological approaches for generating counterfactual explanations have been proposed in the literature. One of the most popular is the DiCE framework \cite{mothilal2020explaining}, an optimization-based method, which formulate counterfactual generation as a constrained optimization problem that seeks minimal changes to input features while achieving a desired prediction;

The integration of machine learning and counterfactual explanations offers a promising framework for both the prediction and interpretation of diabetic peripheral neuropathy. By combining high predictive accuracy with actionable, patient-specific insights, this approach has the potential to support clinicians in early diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment planning. Moreover, it bridges the gap between advanced computational models and practical clinical utility, fostering greater trust and adoption of AI-driven tools in healthcare. 

This study aims to investigate the application of machine learning models in conjunction with counterfactual explanation techniques for the prediction and interpretation of diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) risk, thereby contributing to the growing body of research on interpretable and actionable artificial intelligence in medicine. The study also examines the potential insights derived from incorporating Sudoscan measurements---a modality that is comparatively underrepresented in many AI-based DPN studies, which often rely primarily on routine clinical and demographic variables---while deliberately excluding nerve conduction study results from the predictor set, since these constitute the diagnostic reference standard that the model is intended to triage patients toward. Furthermore, by utilizing data collected within the Philippine clinical context, this work enhances the geographic diversity of current studies and provides context-specific insights into the application of explainable AI for DPN.

\section{Methods}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{methods}

\subsection{Study Population and Data Collection}
%************************************************
\label{sec:cohort}

A total of 201 adult Filipino patients with diabetes mellitus were recruited at a tertiary hospital
in Quezon City, Philippines between 2022 and 2023. Patients with limb amputation, gangrenous or
necrotic foot, implanted cardiac pacemakers or defibrillators, or other known causes of
polyneuropathy were excluded, leaving 190 enrolled; a further 3 were dropped for incomplete
measurements, giving 187 analyzed patients (Figure~\ref{fig:patient_flow_classification}). Each
underwent the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI) in its Filipino-validated form
\cite{dagang2016validation}, a bedside neurological examination, Sudoscan sudomotor testing, and
lower-extremity nerve conduction studies (NCS), and was classified under the 2009 Toronto consensus
criteria as confirmed, probable, possible, or no DPN \cite{tesfaye2010diabetic}. Because the study
targets pre-diagnostic screening, the latter three categories---whose DPN status cannot be
established without further electrodiagnostic evaluation---were collapsed into a single Unconfirmed
class, giving a binary target of 130 Confirmed and 57 Unconfirmed patients. This reflects the
clinical objective: identify patients with a high probability of DPN and prioritize them for NCS
referral where that test is costly or inaccessible. The study was approved by the local
Institutional Ethics Review Board and all subjects gave written consent.

The bedside examination used the 10 g Semmes--Weinstein monofilament, a 128 Hz tuning fork, pinprick
testing, and Achilles tendon reflex testing dichotomized as normal versus hypo/areflexive
\cite{feng2009semmes,oyer2007quantitative}; Sudoscan measured average electrochemical skin
conductance at the hands and feet by reverse iontophoresis and chronoamperometry, a noninvasive
surrogate for sudomotor function \cite{garcia2022detection}. All neurological examinations were
performed by a single trained physician and all NCS by a single trained technician on the same day.
Full acquisition protocols, including NCS equipment settings and the nerve attributes measured, are
given in Additional file~1. \textbf{The NCS measurements are the diagnostic reference standard from
which the label is derived and are excluded from every predictor set at every stage}
(Section~\ref{sec:leakage}); the 22 candidate predictors available to the models are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:cf_features}.

\subsection{Machine Learning Modeling}
%*************************************

\subsubsection{Models}
%=====================
\label{sec:models}

Fourteen algorithms spanning linear and probabilistic models (logistic regression, linear
discriminant analysis, quadratic discriminant analysis, stochastic gradient descent, Gaussian naive
Bayes, linear support vector machine), instance-based learning (k-nearest neighbors), kernel methods
(RBF support vector machine), tree ensembles (decision tree, random forest, extra trees, gradient
boosting), and advanced gradient boosting frameworks (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) were evaluated
under default or standard hyperparameters, together with a naive classifier predicting the majority
class as a baseline. This spread covers linear, nonlinear, probabilistic, and ensemble paradigms,
which differ in interpretability, flexibility, and capacity; a description of each is given in
Additional file~1.

\subsubsection{Model and Feature Set Selection}
%==============================================
\label{sec:modelselection}

Given the limited number of Unconfirmed cases, the fourteen algorithms were benchmarked under
repeated stratified $k$-fold cross-validation---4 folds $\times$ 10 repetitions, for 40 validation
runs per model---against each of twelve feature sets, giving $14 \times 12 \times 40$ scored runs.
Stratification preserves the class distribution in every fold; repetition reduces the variance of
the resulting estimate. The twelve sets comprise the full set (All); a reduced set dropping features
with a variance inflation factor above 5 (NoCol); five ablations removing one feature group at a
time (NoProf, NoCom, NoNeuro, NoMSI, NoSudo); Sudoscan alone (Sudo); and four additive sets pairing
Sudoscan with one other group (SudoProf, SudoCom, SudoNeuro, SudoMnsi). The ablations identify which
groups contribute most to predictive performance, while the Sudoscan-anchored additive sets quantify
its complementary value given its established role in peripheral neuropathy screening.

For every model--feature-set combination the 40 per-run scores were summarized by their mean, and
models ranked by mean area under the precision--recall curve (AUPRC), which directly summarizes the
trade-off defining the intended screening use (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). CatBoost on the full
feature set was carried forward to hyperparameter optimization (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}).

\subsubsection{Performance Metrics}
%==================================
\label{sec:metrics}
For each of the 40 validation runs, eight metrics were recorded: accuracy, precision, sensitivity
(recall), specificity, the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity $-$ 1) \cite{youden1950index}, F1,
ROC-AUC, and AUPRC. Sensitivity and specificity jointly
express the clinical trade-off of a pre-diagnostic screening tool, while AUPRC---the ranking
metric---was prioritized over ROC-AUC because its two axes are the quantities that govern the
intended use \cite{saito2015precision,davis2006relationship}. With Confirmed DPN as the positive class, recall is the fraction of confirmed patients
the screen refers onward for NCS, and precision is the positive predictive value of that
referral---the fraction of referrals to a resource-intensive and often geographically inaccessible
test that prove justified. AUPRC summarizes this trade-off across all thresholds and is anchored to
the cohort's Confirmed prevalence of 0.695, the no-skill baseline against which reported values
should be read. ROC-AUC instead plots recall against the false-positive rate, a quantity not acted
upon at the point of referral, and is by construction invariant to prevalence.

\subsubsection{Preventing Data Leakage}
%======================================
\label{sec:leakage}

Every stage enforces a strict separation between the data used to fit or tune a model and the data
used to evaluate it. Most importantly, \textit{the NCS variables are excluded from every modeling
stage, before selection begins}. These measurements are the reference standard from which the
Confirmed/Unconfirmed label is derived under the Toronto criteria, so admitting them as predictors
would leak the outcome into the feature set; their exclusion is also what makes the model usable for
its intended purpose, screening where NCS is unavailable. The 22 candidate predictors of
Table~\ref{tab:cf_features} are the only variables any model in this study sees.

Beyond the predictor set, each of the 40 repeated-CV runs fits on a training fold and scores on a
disjoint test fold; the nested cross-validation of Section~\ref{sec:hpo} confines hyperparameter
search, refitting, and threshold selection entirely to each split's training set, touching the test
set exactly once after tuning is complete; and the explainability and counterfactual stages reuse
the training-set models and split-specific thresholds already fitted during optimization, never
refitting against or selecting a threshold from a test set. CatBoost's feature importances are the
one exception: they are read off the fitted trees rather than from held-out predictions, and so
describe each split's training set by construction.

\subsubsection{Handling Class Imbalance and a Small Sample Size}
%===============================================================
\label{sec:classimbalance}

The cohort is small by machine-learning standards (187 patients, 22 candidate predictors) and
imbalanced (130 Confirmed vs.\ 57 Unconfirmed). Repeated stratified cross-validation is the primary
defense against the variance a small $n$ introduces into any single estimate: stratification
constrains every fold to reproduce the cohort's class distribution, and repetition averages out the
sampling variability of any one partition. Class imbalance itself is handled inside the model rather
than by resampling: CatBoost's positive-class weight (\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}) was tuned per
train/test split over 0.3--1.0 alongside the other hyperparameters (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}). The
Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique was deliberately not used---with only 57 Unconfirmed
patients across 22 features, its nearest-neighbor interpolation would synthesize clinically
fabricated patients in an already sparse feature space. Reweighting the loss keeps every training
example a real patient, which matters for the grounding of the downstream counterfactual task.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Optimization}
%==========================================
\label{sec:hpo}

CatBoost was carried forward to nested cross-validation hyperparameter optimization
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview}). An outer repeated stratified $k$-fold partitioning---$k=4$
folds $\times$ 10 repeats under a fixed random seed---divides the cohort into 40 \textit{train/test
splits}, over which final performance is reported; nested inside each, a second stratified $k$-fold
partitioning ($k=3$) subdivides that split's \textit{training set} alone into \textit{tuning folds}
used exclusively for hyperparameter search.

Within each train/test split, an Optuna Tree-structured Parzen Estimator sampler ran 100 trials,
each fitting three CatBoost models---one per tuning fold---and scored by mean AUPRC across the three
held-out tuning folds. Four hyperparameters were searched: tree depth (4--10), learning rate
(0.003--0.3, log-uniform), $L_2$ leaf regularization (1.0--10.0), and the positive-class weight
(0.3--1.0). All other settings were held constant: logistic loss, AUC as evaluation metric, at most
500 boosting iterations, and early stopping after 50 rounds without improvement. The winning trial's
parameters were refit on the full training set and separately used for three out-of-fold refits
over the same tuning folds, yielding one held-out probability per training patient; a classification
threshold was then selected per split as the value maximizing F1 on the precision--recall curve of
those out-of-fold probabilities---never on the test set. The refit model and its threshold were
applied once to the untouched test set. Per-split values are pooled across all 40 splits as a mean
$\pm$ 95\% confidence interval (normal approximation, $z=1.96$).

This is \textit{one} modeling pipeline---one algorithm, one search procedure---evaluated by 4-fold
cross-validation repeated 10 times, not forty separate studies, and its primary performance claim is
the single pooled mean over the 40 splits. The four fold models of the \textit{first} repeat, with
their partitions, tuned hyperparameters and thresholds, are additionally saved for reuse without
refitting by the explainability and counterfactual stages; references below to ``model~0'' through
``model~3'' denote these. The counterfactual stage uses them directly, while the explainability
stage refits each on its own saved training set under that fold's tuned configuration and original
seed---reproducing the saved model exactly---so that attributions can be reported against named
clinical variables rather than anonymous input positions.

\subsection{Explainability Analysis}
%***********************************
\label{sec:explainability}

Post-hoc explainability is computed on the four retrained fold models and reported fold by fold,
never averaged into one ranking: four separately grown ensembles, each under its own tuned
configuration, admit no meaningful mean. What the folds agree on---which features occupy the upper
tier, rather than their exact order within it---is the stability evidence being claimed.

\textit{CatBoost feature importance} is taken from the built-in PredictionValuesChange criterion,
which scores a feature by how much the ensemble's output shifts on average as that feature's split
conditions change, normalized to sum to 100 across the 22 features. It is read off the fitted trees,
so no test data enters the computation. \textit{SHAP attributions} are computed for each fold model
on that fold's held-out test set only, wrapping the model's positive-class probability as a
black-box function and using the same test fold as background distribution; for each feature the
mean absolute SHAP value across the fold's test patients is reported. The two are comparable in
ranking but not in value: importances are order $10$ on a scale summing to 100, SHAP magnitudes
order $10^{-2}$ on the probability scale. Neither answers whether a feature can be acted upon, which
is what the counterfactual stage is for.

\subsection{Counterfactual Generation}
%*************************************

Counterfactual explanations were generated with the DiCE (Diverse Counterfactual Explanations)
framework \cite{mothilal2020explaining}, chosen for its model-agnostic operation, its
diversity-aware search---which surfaces multiple plausible feature modifications rather than one---and
its support for feature constraints such as permitted ranges and immutable variables.

\subsubsection{Feature Suitability as Counterfactuals}
%=====================================================
Features were stratified by actionability before generation (Table~\ref{tab:cf_features}). Sudoscan
readings are unsuitable because they are biomarker outcomes of neuropathy rather than inputs a
clinician can act on, as are neurological examination findings and the MNSI score; demographic
attributes and diabetes duration are immutable. Two features are marked \textit{conditional}---peripheral
arterial occlusive disease and chronic kidney disease---being partially manageable but
not fully reversible. This leaves six features permitted to vary: HbA1c, insulin use, hypertension,
dyslipidemia, CKD, and PAOD.

\subsubsection{General Setup}
%============================
\label{sec:generalsetup}
Counterfactuals are generated separately for each of the four saved fold models, each wrapped with
\textit{that fold's own} tuned decision threshold rather than one shared across folds. A genetic
search was used rather than a gradient method because of the categorical features, and because it
explores high-dimensional spaces under multiple constraints effectively.

To keep proposals feasible, a global permitted range was defined for each continuous feature over
the whole cohort as $[\max(0,\ x_{min} - \sigma),\ x_{max} + \sigma]$, where $\sigma$ is that
feature's standard deviation; bounding at 0 keeps naturally non-negative quantities positive, and
the $\pm\sigma$ margin allows a value slightly beyond the observed range while remaining plausible.

\subsubsection{Local Counterfactual Analysis}
%============================================
Within each fold's test set, counterfactuals were generated for \textit{misclassified} instances and
for \textit{borderline} instances, defined as those whose predicted probability lies within
$\delta = 0.08$ of that fold's own tuned threshold; these are the cases where a small feature change
may alter the outcome. For each selected instance an instance-specific permitted range was defined
as $[\max(0,\ x_i - \sigma),\ x_i + \sigma]$---centered on the patient's own value rather than the
cohort mean, so a proposed change is scaled to a magnitude seen in the cohort but anchored where
that patient starts from.

Up to 20 counterfactuals targeting the opposite class were requested per instance, with proximity
weight 0.5, diversity weight 1.0, categorical penalty 0.1, and post-hoc sparsity pruning (binary
search, parameter 0.05). The relatively high diversity weight is deliberate: with only six features
eligible to vary, the search must be pushed to explore genuinely different combinations rather than
converging on near-duplicates of one edit. Full DiCE configuration is given in Additional file~1,
Additional file~1, Table~\ref{tab:dice_setup}.

\section{Results}\label{results}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This section reports the findings of the four modeling stages in the order the pipeline runs them: the baseline benchmark of candidate models and feature sets, the nested cross-validated hyperparameter optimization of the selected model, the feature importance and SHAP attributions of the resulting fold models, and the counterfactual explanations generated for individual patients. Their interpretation---the rationale behind the metric and model choices, the clinical reading of each finding, and the study's limitations---is taken up in Section~\ref{discussion}.

\subsection{Baseline Model and Feature Set Evaluation}
%*******************************************************
\label{sec:results-selection}

Across all 40 runs on the full feature set, ensemble tree-based methods consistently outperformed other model families (Additional file~1, Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics}). Random Forest attained the highest mean AUPRC (0.892 $\pm$ 0.039), narrowly ahead of CatBoost (0.889 $\pm$ 0.044) and Extra Trees (0.885 $\pm$ 0.042); the three are statistically indistinguishable, with all three means falling well within one standard deviation of one another. Naive Bayes (0.884), Logistic Regression (0.880), RBF SVM (0.878), and LDA (0.878) followed closely behind, underscoring that the full feature set carries strong, model-agnostic signal for DPN status rather than favoring any single learning paradigm.

Random Forest and CatBoost occupy essentially the same operating point: sensitivity of 0.916 and 0.913, and specificity of 0.529 and 0.512, respectively. RBF SVM achieved the highest sensitivity overall (0.938), but at the cost of the lowest specificity among the top models (0.453).

Turning from models to feature groups, the heatmap of average AUPRC across feature sets (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_heatmaps}) shows three patterns. First, the top models (Random Forest, CatBoost, Extra Trees) are remarkably robust to the removal of most feature groups: ablating Clinical Profile (NoProf), Comorbidities (NoCom), or MNSI (NoMsi) produced negligible or no AUPRC change for these models (all within 0.01 of the full-feature-set value). The exception is Neurological Examination features (NoNeuro), whose removal produced the largest drop observed for any ablation among the top models (Random Forest and CatBoost both fall to 0.86), suggesting these bedside signs are the least redundant predictors in the set.

The Sudoscan-only feature set (Sudo) understandably lowered performance for every model (Random Forest 0.82, CatBoost 0.83), while the additive Sudoscan-anchored feature sets (SudoProf, SudoCom, SudoNeuro, SudoMnsi) demonstrated modest but inconsistent improvements over Sudoscan-only, suggesting diminishing returns from adding other feature groups once Sudoscan data are available. One exception worth noting is that the SudoNeuro combination (Sudoscan with Neurological Examination findings) recovered to within 0.01 of full-feature-set performance for both top models (0.89 Random Forest, 0.90 CatBoost).

We note that since these metrics are based on default hyperparameters, a more complete picture of feature importance will be explored during the hyperparameter optimization and explainability analysis that follow.

Beyond the mean values, the spread of these estimates matters. The violin plot distributions (Additional file~1, Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_violins}) show that the top models maintain elevated, largely overlapping AUPRC distributions. Random Forest shows the highest median and a comparatively tight interquartile range; CatBoost's median and box are close behind, but with a longer lower tail reaching toward 0.75, indicating an occasional weaker fold rather than a systematically less stable ranking.

\subsection{CatBoost Hyperparameter Optimization}
%*************************************************
\label{sec:results-optimization}
This subsection reports the results of the nested cross-validation hyperparameter optimization procedure for CatBoost on the full feature set, whose design---the 4-fold $\times$ 10-repeat train/test scheme, the 3-fold Optuna TPE search run inside each training set, and the out-of-fold threshold selection that keeps each test set untouched until it is scored exactly once---is described in full in Section~\ref{sec:hpo}. We first report CatBoost's overall performance across the pipeline's 4-fold structure using the full repeated cross-validation scheme (Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}, pooling all 40 train/test splits), then the four individually-tuned fold models from the first of the ten repeats (Additional file~1, Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}) that are saved for reuse, without refitting, by the explainability and counterfactual analyses below.

The mean AUPRC across all 40 runs was 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.871--0.902, STD = 0.049), with a confidence interval spanning just 3.1 percentage points, indicating that model performance is highly reproducible across varying data splits, an important criterion for clinical tool reliability \cite{steyerberg2010assessing}.

The mean sensitivity of 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.861--0.912)---the most clinically critical metric for a pre-diagnostic screening instrument---remained high and reasonably well-constrained, with an 95\% CI spanning roughly 5 percentage points and a STD of 0.083. This indicates that the model's ability to correctly identify DPN-positive patients is comparatively robust to partition variability. By contrast, specificity showed the greatest instability across runs (mean = 0.527, STD = 0.141, 95\% CI: 0.483--0.571), confirming that the model's ability to correctly exclude DPN-negative patients was more sensitive to partition composition. This reflects the relatively smaller size of the Unconfirmed-DPN class (n = 57) and, as the per-fold analysis below shows, fold-by-fold variability in both Bayesian threshold selection and tuned hyperparameters.

The mean F1-score of 0.845 (95\% CI: 0.831--0.860) summarizes the balance between precision and sensitivity in a single number, and its reasonably narrow confidence interval indicates this balance is stable across partitions.

The mean Youden Index of 0.413 (95\% CI: 0.370--0.456, STD = 0.138) was the most variable metric in relative terms, consistent with its dependence on both sensitivity and specificity, the latter being inherently unstable in imbalanced settings. Nevertheless, a Youden Index above 0.37 across the full confidence interval confirms that the model operates meaningfully above chance discrimination at the selected thresholds.

\subsection{Feature Importance and SHAP Attributions}
%****************************************************
\label{sec:results-importance}
Across all four folds, CatBoost feature importances (Figure~\ref{fig:explainability}a) and SHAP-based explanations (Figure~\ref{fig:explainability}b) converged on a consistent set of predictors.

Five predictors occupied the upper tier of the importance ranking in every fold, although their relative order varied from fold to fold: decreased ankle reflex (DEC\_AR; importance 8.35--13.82, SHAP 0.047--0.079), diabetes mellitus duration (DM\_DUR; importance 7.71--11.62, SHAP 0.017--0.059), feet Sudoscan asymmetry (FEET\_PCT\_ASYM; importance 7.02--11.74, SHAP 0.010--0.048), decreased vibration sensation (DEC\_VS; importance 4.64--9.81, SHAP 0.019--0.068), and the MNSI score (importance 5.35--10.55, SHAP 0.013--0.047). Only DEC\_AR placed among the three most important features in all four folds (ranks 2, 3, 3, 1 in Models 0--3), with DM\_DUR next most stable (ranks 4, 1, 1, 3). The remaining three were substantially more fold-dependent: FEET\_PCT\_ASYM (ranks 1, 6, 2, 6), DEC\_VS (ranks 3, 4, 5, 12), and MNSI (ranks 9, 2, 7, 2), the last reaching an importance of 10.55 in Model 1---behind only DM\_DUR's 11.62 in that fold, and ahead of DEC\_AR's 9.87. The consistent membership of this group, rather than any fixed ordering within it, is what the four folds agree on.

Beyond this top group, the Sudoscan-derived cluster contributed substantial predictive weight in aggregate: the neuropathy score (NS; importance 6.68--7.88, SHAP 0.021--0.042), hand Sudoscan asymmetry (HAND\_PCT\_ASYM; importance 5.27--7.52, SHAP 0.008--0.032), and the cardiac autonomic score (CAS; importance 5.52--6.84, SHAP 0.014--0.017) each ranked in the upper half of the feature set in every fold, with foot- and hand-level mean electrochemical skin conductance (FEET\_MEAN\_ESC, HAND\_MEAN\_ESC) contributing more moderately.

HbA1c (HBA1C; importance 5.21--8.13, SHAP 0.007--0.025), age (AGE; importance 5.51--6.48, SHAP 0.014--0.028), and insulin use (INSULIN; importance 1.08--2.58, SHAP 0.004--0.009) contributed consistently but at lower magnitudes than the top predictors above.

In contrast, the comorbidities hypertension (HPN) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) received consistently low feature importance and SHAP values across all four folds, while peripheral arterial occlusive disease (PAOD) and Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) received near-zero importance in every fold.

Her counterfactuals are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:localcf-cases}b.

The 24 generated counterfactuals show what it would take to move her below the threshold. HbA1c is changed in every single one of the 24 counterfactuals, the only patient in this study for which that is true, and the single most parsimonious counterfactual overall is a 0.5-point HbA1c reduction (7.5\% $\to$ 7.0\%) with no other change---the smallest single-feature perturbation observed for any patient (L1 = 0.5). Two-feature combinations largely pair a similar HbA1c reduction with stopping insulin (18 of 24 counterfactuals touch insulin) or resolving PAOD (6 of 24); hypertension and CKD are altered less often (4 and 2 instances). Two counterfactuals instead moved the immutable SUBJ feature alongside a larger HbA1c reduction and were filtered out as unactionable.

The third case, Patient 123 under Model 1, is a confident false negative. She is female, 68 years old; diabetes duration 3 years; HbA1c 7.18\%; not on insulin; hypertension present; no PAOD, dyslipidemia, or CKD. Incorrectly predicted as Unconfirmed DPN at a probability of 0.269, well below Model 1's threshold of 0.5 (margin = 0.231), despite a confirmed clinical diagnosis. Her counterfactuals are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:localcf-cases}c.

She is the only false negative among the eight successful instances and, at a margin of 0.231, the least borderline case in this study---Model 1 places her confidently in the wrong class. The 24 counterfactuals generated for her describe what would need to be different for the model to correctly flag her as Confirmed DPN, and every one of them changes HbA1c downward (typically by 1.2--2.2 points, e.g., 7.18\% $\to$ 5.0\%), the largest and most consistent single driver of any patient in this study; hypertension is the second most frequent change (removed in 15 of 24), followed by PAOD (introduced in 11 of 24), insulin (started in 7 of 24), dyslipidemia (introduced in 2 of 24), and CKD (introduced in 1 of 24).

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This study is a proof-of-concept investigation into whether counterfactual explanations can make a
machine-learning DPN screening model actionable for clinicians, not a validated or deployment-ready
clinical instrument. To our knowledge it is among the first to systematically integrate
counterfactual explanations into a pre-diagnostic DPN screening pipeline intended for low-resource
settings where nerve conduction studies are costly or geographically inaccessible; the counterfactual
analysis, rather than the classifier's raw discriminative performance, is the primary contribution.
We interpret below the model and feature-set selection, the optimized model's operating point, the
convergence of feature importance and SHAP, and then the counterfactual analysis that is this
study's centre---setting out how a clinical counterfactual should be read, applying that framework to
three cases, and synthesizing the aggregate patterns. We close with a proposed clinical workflow and
the study's limitations.

\subsection{Feature Set and Model Selection}
%*******************************************
\label{sec:catboost_vs_rf}

The naive baseline predicting the majority class achieved an AUPRC of 0.695---essentially the
empirical prevalence---despite perfect sensitivity and 69.5\% accuracy, illustrating why accuracy and
sensitivity alone are insufficient criteria in an imbalanced screening task. Against that baseline
the top three ensemble methods are statistically indistinguishable, so the choice between Random
Forest and CatBoost cannot rest on the baseline ranking alone; the grounds that decided it are set
out at the end of this subsection. The full feature set was retained for all subsequent stages.

The robustness of the top models to most feature-group ablations (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_heatmaps})
implies that no single group is indispensable and that redundancy across the feature space is
substantial. The exception is informative: neurological examination findings were the least redundant
group, and the SudoNeuro recovery indicates that simple bedside signs---vibration sensation, pinprick,
light touch, and ankle reflex---complement electrochemical skin conductance synergistically. A reduced
Sudoscan-plus-examination panel is therefore a viable lower-burden alternative to the full feature set
where a complete profile, comorbidity and MNSI workup is impractical. That these estimates come from
40 partitions rather than one matters here: with a single k-fold split, estimates on a cohort this
size can vary substantially with the partition drawn \cite{kim2009estimating}.

Two considerations the baseline table cannot capture then favor CatBoost over Random Forest. First, small-sample
regularization: with roughly 140 patients per training set, CatBoost's ordered boosting computes each
tree's residuals on a permutation-restricted subset excluding the point being scored, guarding
against the target leakage plain gradient boosting is prone to, and its symmetric tree structure
further restricts the hypothesis space in a way suited to small $n$. Its native handling of
categorical comorbidity flags and its class reweighting also avoid the one-hot dimensionality
inflation and synthetic-patient fabrication that would compound on a cohort this small
\cite{dorogush2018catboost}. Second, and more decisively, compatibility with the counterfactual
search: DiCE's genetic algorithm treats the model as a black box and drives its fitness function from
the predicted class probability. Random Forest's probability is a vote-share average over leaf-class
proportions; on a small, high-purity dataset many leaves become pure, producing flat regions with
little gradient signal for the search to climb. CatBoost's additive sum of shallow trees passed
through a sigmoid gives a smoother, higher-resolution surface, which is exactly what the ``minimal
sufficient change'' logic of a clinically usable counterfactual depends on.

\subsection{CatBoost Model Training and Optimization}
%****************************************************
\label{sec:foldchoice}

That sensitivity is comparatively robust to partition variability is of direct clinical relevance:
where the consequence of a missed case includes delayed intervention, progressive nerve damage, and
increased risk of ulceration or amputation, a consistently high sensitivity is more valuable than a
high average with wide variability \cite{tesfaye2010diabetic}. Specificity, the least stable metric,
is correspondingly the pipeline's clearest weakness, and the wide interval on the Youden index
confirms that composite balance metrics are more partition-sensitive than sensitivity-dominant ones
in this class distribution. Establishing stability across 40 partitions before examining any one set
of fold models avoids drawing conclusions from a fold-specific artifact, but it is not on its own
evidence of readiness for deployment, which would additionally require external validation
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

That leaves the question of which concrete models to carry into the stages that follow. SHAP
attributions and counterfactuals must be computed against fitted models, and no
meaningful ``average model'' can be built by averaging 40 independently grown ensembles with 40
different tuned configurations, so one repeat's four fold models must be selected and reused. We use
the first repeat, fixed by the pipeline's random seed rather than chosen after comparing outcomes, so
it cannot reflect cherry-picking; its per-fold means fall within roughly one standard deviation of
the pooled 40-repeat estimates for every metric. This is a plausibility check rather than a
guarantee---Model 0's comparatively low sensitivity is one place where this repeat shows fold-specific
behavior worth keeping in mind when reading the results that build on it.

\subsection{Feature Importance and Model Interpretability}
%*********************************************************
The convergence of CatBoost importances and SHAP attributions across all four independently trained
folds lends biological plausibility to the model's decision logic, a critical consideration for
clinical acceptance \cite{rudin2019}. The composition of the upper tier is clinically coherent: loss
of ankle reflex and impaired vibration perception are among the earliest and most reliable
large-fiber signs of DPN, and long-standing hyperglycemia, indexed by diabetes duration, is a
well-established driver of cumulative nerve damage \cite{tesfaye2010diabetic}. Sudoscan measures
small-fiber sudomotor function, an early manifestation that frequently precedes or accompanies the
large-fiber involvement detectable by NCS \cite{casellini2013sudoscan}; the co-prominence of
examination findings, diabetes duration, and multiple Sudoscan parameters across folds suggests the
model integrates complementary large- and small-fiber information rather than relying on a single
modality---consistent with the SudoNeuro ablation result.

This is also the precise point at which importance-based explanation stops being useful to a
clinician. Every one of those top predictors is a measurement of established neuropathic state, not a
lever anyone can pull. HbA1c and insulin use are comparatively modest drivers of overall importance,
yet---because they are the features amenable to intervention---they dominate the counterfactual
recipes below. Importance and counterfactual salience answer different questions, what the model
relies on across the cohort versus what can be changed for a given patient, and are not expected to
rank features identically. Counterfactual analysis exists in this study to close that gap.

\subsection{Counterfactual Analysis}
%***********************************
\label{sec:counterfactual}\label{sec:cf-plausibility}
Counterfactual explanations ask what minimal change to a patient's features would produce a different
prediction. Unlike global methods such as SHAP, which describe average model behavior, they are
patient-specific and action-oriented, which suits clinical decision support \cite{wachter2017}. A
prerequisite for clinical meaning is that altered features correspond to realistically achievable
interventions, which is why features were stratified by actionability before generation
(Table~\ref{tab:cf_features}); counterfactuals built on non-actionable features risk explanations
that are statistically valid but clinically misleading \cite{karimi2020}. Being on the actionable
list is necessary but not sufficient, however: DiCE is free to propose \textit{introducing} a
comorbidity such as PAOD or CKD, since an actionable feature can move in either direction, even
though inducing a new disease is never an intervention.

That only 8 of 61 candidates yielded a counterfactual is a documented consequence of how the genetic
search behaves on this dataset, not an arbitrary failure. Separately, the search has an independently
documented tendency to violate the immutability constraint---proposing a change to a feature marked
non-actionable, such as SUBJ or a Sudoscan reading---in a small fraction of counterfactuals for a
given patient, regardless of model or configuration; every counterfactual reported here has been
screened against this constraint and violating ones removed, reported below as a transparency record
rather than silently discarded. Both behaviors stem from the same cause: a genetic search's fitness
landscape can be poorly conditioned in regions of a small, high-dimensional clinical feature space,
and CatBoost's smoother probability surface (Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf}) mitigates but does not
eliminate this fragility. The 8 patients below are therefore not a representative sample of typical
DPN cases; they are the subset the search could solve, and the absence of a counterfactual for any
other candidate is evidence of search difficulty, not evidence that none exists.

\subsection{Instance-Level Counterfactual Analysis}
%**************************************************
\label{sec:instance-level-cf}
Before turning to the cases, it is worth stating how a counterfactual for this task should be read,
because the three differ along two independent axes and conflating them is the likeliest source of
misinterpretation.

The first axis is the \emph{direction of the flip}, which fixes the register in which a change can be
discussed at all. A flip to Unconfirmed DPN targets a state a clinician would welcome, so each change
can be examined as a candidate action: can it be done, and should it be? A flip to Confirmed DPN
admits no action---no clinician intervenes to give a patient neuropathy---and the only available
reading is evidential: what does the change reveal about the associations the model has learned, and
are they clinically plausible? Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading} sets out how each type of change
reads under each direction. The second axis is whether the prediction being flipped was
\emph{correct}, which fixes what the counterfactual set is for. When the prediction is wrong, the flip
moves toward clinical truth and the set diagnoses a model error; when it is right, the flip moves away
from clinical truth and the set instead probes robustness. The axes are independent, and their
independence is easy to overlook: a change can be entirely sensible as a clinical act and still, for a
correctly classified patient, describe an outcome nobody wants. The three cases below occupy three of
the four resulting combinations.

Because Patient 20 (Model 3) is a borderline false positive, her counterfactuals flip \emph{toward} the correct label:
they describe what the model would have needed to see in order not to raise a false alarm, and are
read as a diagnosis of its error rather than as advice. With so small a margin and such sparse
counterfactuals, the case shows exactly which evidence tipped the model over: a markedly elevated
HbA1c of 10.3\% in a patient with only 1 year of diabetes, reinforced by insulin use and dyslipidemia,
any one of which is enough to reverse the call. Those three do not read the same way. Reducing HbA1c
and controlling dyslipidemia are enactable and advisable; stopping insulin, in half her
counterfactuals, is enactable but inadvisable and would be inappropriate to act on at this HbA1c.
Because her DPN is not confirmed, the advisable subset is at least harmless---but that is a
consequence of her being a false positive, not a warrant carried by the counterfactual, and the same
changes for a correctly flagged patient carry the opposite implication. What the counterfactual
contributes is transparency about why the tool flagged her.

Patient 40 (Model 0) inverts the situation: as a borderline true positive, her counterfactuals flip \emph{away} from the
correct label, so they cannot be read as a route to a better outcome. What they measure is how much
supporting evidence would have to be withdrawn before the model changed its mind. Her set makes the
two axes visibly independent. Eighteen of 24 counterfactuals involve stopping insulin---enactable but
inadvisable on its own terms, and unusable without any appeal to her having been classified
correctly. The most parsimonious proposal is the opposite case: a 0.5-point HbA1c reduction is exactly
what standard diabetes care pursues, and yet its effect here is one nobody would want, because it
unflags a patient whose DPN is confirmed. Most of the set fails as advice on clinical grounds; its
best-behaved member fails on what it accomplishes. The practical implication is for the tool, not the
care plan: a 0.5-point reduction would not reverse a confirmed diagnosis, but it moves her from
flagged to unflagged, so correct positives this close to the boundary should be treated as provisional
and confirmed by examination.

Patient 123 (Model 1) is a confident false negative, so her counterfactuals flip \emph{toward} the correct label and
describe a model deficit---the evidence it would have needed to catch a patient it missed. Because
this flip targets Confirmed DPN, none is an intervention proposal; the only question is whether the
implied association is plausible, and her set divides. The comorbidity introductions fall on the
plausible side: PAOD, dyslipidemia and CKD are recognized DPN risk factors, so a model raising its
probability in their presence has learned something coherent, even though introducing them is not
something that can be done. Starting insulin is likewise hypothesis-generating, consistent with
insulin marking longer-standing or poorer-controlled diabetes. The HbA1c and hypertension changes do
not survive: every counterfactual lowers her HbA1c and 15 of 24 remove her hypertension, implying that
better glycemic control and treated blood pressure \emph{raise} DPN probability---the reverse of the
established risk direction, and certainly not an argument for relaxing either. Magnitude compounds
this: a shift from 7.18\% to 5.0\% is not merely inverted but larger than a plausible clinical
trajectory. Within this partition, Model 1 has learned a defensible comorbidity signal alongside an
inverted glycemic and blood-pressure one, and it is the inverted component that dominates and leaves
her on the unconfirmed side. This is the case that most directly illustrates the stakes of a false
negative: a patient with confirmed DPN, comfortably below the referral threshold, for whom the
counterfactual exposes where the model's learned associations diverge from clinical ones.

Taken together, the three cases show a common structure. Across all three, HbA1c is the feature the model is most sensitive to, echoing its role in the
aggregate analysis below; insulin and, for Patient 123, hypertension follow. The artifacts a human
reader must filter recur in three kinds: changes that cannot be enacted at all (introducing a
comorbidity, or moving an immutable feature), changes that could be enacted but should not be
(stopping insulin), and changes whose implied association is inverted (Patient 123's HbA1c and
hypertension). Because a counterfactual state is reached only if all its changes occur together, a
single member of any of these kinds disqualifies the whole counterfactual rather than the offending
feature alone. A generated counterfactual is a candidate explanation for clinical review, not an
automatic prescription.

\subsection{Aggregate-Level Counterfactual Analysis}
%***************************************************
\label{sec:aggregate-cf}
The dominance of HbA1c across the aggregate set is clinically coherent and well supported: chronic
hyperglycemia drives the oxidative and metabolic mechanisms underlying peripheral nerve damage, and
tighter control reduces DPN incidence and progression \cite{callaghan2012,tesfaye2010diabetic}. The
counterfactual analysis does not merely identify HbA1c as the most frequently implicated actionable
feature but operationalizes it as a concrete recommendation: patients flagged as high-risk may be
redirected toward glycemic optimization, insulin therapy review, and dyslipidemia management, in
roughly that order of frequency, before or alongside NCS referral. Combined with the low mean
sparsity, the feature-frequency pattern suggests the models rely on a small number of co-occurring
risk factors---most often HbA1c with one of insulin, dyslipidemia, or hypertension---rather than a
single threshold or the full comorbidity profile, consistent with the known multifactorial
pathogenesis of DPN \cite{feldman2019}. The substantial representation of dyslipidemia and
hypertension is consistent with their established roles as risk amplifiers through vascular and
metabolic mechanisms \cite{jaiswal2017,callaghan2012}.

Model-level heterogeneity (Figure~\ref{fig:globalcf}) reinforces a point already made: each fold is a
different sample of the same cohort, and its counterfactual signature reflects which patients happened
to be borderline or misclassified in that fold, not four independent findings to be reconciled. Model
1's hypertension/PAOD-led pattern describes Patient 123 specifically, not evidence that hypertension
generally outranks HbA1c. More broadly, grounding explanations in actionable features and framing them
as what-if scenarios addresses a critical barrier to adoption: black-box predictions, however accurate,
are unlikely to be acted upon without a justification that aligns with clinical reasoning
\cite{rudin2019}.

\subsection{A Proposed Clinical Usage Workflow}
%**********************************************
These patterns suggest a low-burden way the pipeline could fit an existing diabetes clinic workflow
(Figure~\ref{fig:clinician_workflow}). At a routine visit the clinic already collects the profile,
comorbidity, MNSI and brief examination data the model uses, optionally with Sudoscan; no NCS is
required to produce a risk score. Patients comfortably below threshold need no further action; those
well above it are prioritized for NCS referral directly. For borderline patients---flagged, but close
to the threshold---the counterfactual is reviewed alongside the prediction to identify the smallest
realistic change among the six actionable features that would move them across it. This review
requires a clinician to filter the suggestion: discard what cannot be enacted (introducing a
comorbidity, altering an immutable attribute) or what could be but should not (withdrawing indicated
insulin), and weigh the remainder---typically glycemic control, or management of hypertension and
dyslipidemia---against the broader clinical picture. The case studies add a caution: a flagged patient
may be flagged correctly, in which case an otherwise sensible proposal is a warning about how little
evidence separates the classes near the threshold, not a care plan. Where few actionable pathways
remain, the patient joins those referred for NCS; where they do remain and correspond to a realistic
intervention, the patient is directed toward targeted risk-factor management and re-screened. This is
a proposal for how the outputs could support decision-making, not a validated protocol: the risk score
and the counterfactual are inputs, and the decision remains the clinician's.

\subsection{Limitations}
%***********************
\label{sec:limitations}
The data come from a single clinic and a single cohort (n = 187). Even with repeated stratified
cross-validation, this cannot substitute for external validation on an independent cohort, and the
reported performance and counterfactual patterns may not generalize to populations with different
demographics, comorbidity prevalence, or diagnostic practices. The cohort was not randomly sampled
from a defined population, so selection bias---for instance toward patients already referred for the
work-up that established their DPN status---cannot be ruled out. Threshold-dependent metrics at the
baseline stage used the default 0.5 probability threshold, a suboptimal choice for screening that the
optimization stage addresses but that should temper reading of the baseline comparison.

No prospective testing has been performed: the model and its counterfactuals have not been evaluated
in a live workflow, and it is unknown whether clinicians presented with these outputs would make
different or better referral decisions. Deploying a screening model of this kind carries real risks if
these steps are skipped---a false negative could delay diagnosis, and a false positive could prompt an
unnecessary referral or, more subtly, undue reassurance to a clinician who over-trusts a favorable
counterfactual---which is why we frame this as decision support requiring clinician oversight at every
step rather than an autonomous diagnostic tool. The counterfactual results carry a further caveat:
only 8 of 61 candidates yielded a usable counterfactual and one entire fold yielded none, so the
reported set is what the search could solve rather than a representative sample
(Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}). Finally, the small dataset limits the granularity of subgroup
analysis, contributes to the specificity instability, and is the same underlying reason a subset of
candidates could not be resolved; and the actionable features are encoded largely as binary
comorbidity flags, which limits how finely a proposed change maps onto a titratable clinical target.

Several directions follow. The most urgent is external validation on independent, multi-center
cohorts, followed by prospective clinician-in-the-loop evaluation measuring whether
counterfactual-augmented risk scores change referral decisions and improve the diagnostic yield of the
nerve conduction studies actually ordered. The low yield should be addressed directly, by comparing
alternative generators, by enforcing actionability constraints inside the generator rather than
screening for violations afterward, and by testing whether a larger cohort smooths the poorly
conditioned regions of the fitness landscape. Encoding actionable features as continuous quantities
where they exist---lipid fractions, blood pressure, estimated glomerular filtration rate---would let
counterfactual magnitudes map onto quantitative treatment targets. The reduced Sudoscan-plus-examination
panel deserves prospective evaluation as the lowest-burden configuration identified here.

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

A model trained only on data a diabetes clinic can obtain without electrodiagnostic
equipment---clinical profile, comorbidities, MNSI, a brief bedside neurological examination, and
Sudoscan---identified patients with confirmed diabetic peripheral neuropathy at a mean AUPRC of 0.886
(95\% CI 0.871--0.902) and a mean sensitivity of 0.886 (0.861--0.912) across 40 train/test splits,
against a no-skill baseline of 0.695. Specificity (0.527, 0.483--0.571) was the one unstable metric
and remains the pipeline's clearest weakness. Feature-set ablation produced a result of immediate
practical value: bedside neurological examination was the least redundant modality, and Sudoscan plus
examination alone came within 0.01 AUPRC of the full feature set, making a reduced two-modality panel
a plausible lower-burden configuration.

Feature importance and SHAP converged on decreased ankle reflex, diabetes duration, Sudoscan
asymmetry, and decreased vibration sensation---all markers of established neuropathy rather than
treatable targets. Only counterfactual analysis surfaced the modifiable pathways a clinician can act
on: restricted to the six features for which a clinical pathway exists, DiCE altered HbA1c in 91.1\%
of its 158 counterfactuals but seldom alone, pairing it with insulin use (51.3\%), dyslipidemia
(37.3\%), or hypertension (31.0\%). The picture is multifactorial rather than glycemia-only, and the
features implicated most often map directly onto the targets of existing diabetes complication
management guidelines \cite{ada2024}. Alongside this, the study contributes a reading framework for clinical
counterfactuals---the two axes of flip direction and prediction correctness, and the
action-versus-evidence register they define---which generalizes beyond DPN to any clinical
counterfactual application where a proposed change may be unenactable, inadvisable, or directionally
implausible.

Screening without electrodiagnostic input therefore reached a sensitivity suitable for triage, limited
by unstable specificity, in a single-center proof-of-concept that requires external and prospective
validation before any clinical use. Every counterfactual it produces is a candidate explanation for
clinician review, not a prescription.

\subsection{Detailed clinical assessment protocol}

Neuropathic symptoms were documented by asking patients about distal extremity numbness,
paresthesia, or pain, and by administering the Filipino-validated Michigan Neuropathy Screening
Instrument \cite{dagang2016validation}. Abnormal neurological signs were detected using the standard
10 g Semmes--Weinstein monofilament examination (SWME) and standard 128 Hz clanging tuning fork tests
\cite{feng2009semmes,oyer2007quantitative}; pinprick sensation was examined under the same criteria
as SWME. Achilles tendon reflex testing dichotomized subjects as normal or hypo/areflexive. All
neurological examinations were conducted by a single trained physician.

Nerve conduction studies were performed at a stable skin temperature of 32\textdegree C and a room
temperature of 24\textdegree C using Nihon Kohden EMG equipment with a filter width of 20 Hz to
3 kHz, amplifier setting at 200 $\mu$V/div, and sweep speed of 10 ms/div. The following nerve
attributes were measured: sural sensory nerve action potentials (SNAP) and conduction velocities
(CV); superficial peroneal SNAP and CV; and tibial motor nerve compound muscle action potentials
(CMAP), CV, distal latencies and F-wave latencies. All NCS testing was done by a single trained
technician on the same day as the other neurological examinations. These measurements define the
diagnostic reference standard and are excluded from every predictor set.

Sudomotor testing used Sudoscan: subjects placed the palms of their hands and the soles of their feet
on stainless steel electrodes and an incremental low direct voltage ($<$4 V) was applied for about
2 minutes. The device produces an average electrochemical skin conductance (ESC) for hands and feet,
based on stimulation of sweat glands by the low-level voltage, providing evidence of sweat
dysfunction \cite{garcia2022detection}.

\subsection{Candidate models}

Fourteen algorithms were benchmarked. \textit{Linear and probabilistic models}---logistic regression,
linear discriminant analysis (LDA), quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA), stochastic gradient
descent (SGD), Gaussian naive Bayes, and linear support vector machine---are interpretable and
computationally efficient; logistic regression and linear SVM are effective in high-dimensional
spaces, LDA and QDA model class-conditional distributions with QDA allowing nonlinear boundaries
through class-specific covariance, naive Bayes performs well under conditional independence, and SGD
enables scalable optimization. \textit{k-nearest neighbors} is a non-parametric instance-based method
predicting from proximity in feature space, simple but sensitive to feature scaling and noise.
\textit{Tree-based and ensemble methods}---decision tree, random forest, extra trees, and gradient
boosting---capture nonlinear relationships and feature interactions, with the ensembles improving
generalization through bagging and randomized feature selection. \textit{Advanced gradient boosting
frameworks}---XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost---are optimized for tabular data, with XGBoost
incorporating regularization and sparse-data handling, LightGBM using histogram-based leaf-wise
growth, and CatBoost handling categorical variables and mitigating overfitting through ordered
boosting. The \textit{RBF support vector machine} extends linear SVM with a radial basis kernel. A
\textit{naive} classifier predicting the majority class provides the baseline.

\subsection{Pooled model diagnostics}

Two complementary views of the four fold models' out-of-sample performance are reported in
Figure~\ref{fig:catboost_auprc}. \textit{Per-fold} ROC and precision--recall curves are plotted
separately for each fold model, to show whether discrimination is stable across folds.
\textit{Pooled} diagnostics are computed by concatenating the four fold models' predictions on their
own test sets, giving one out-of-sample prediction per patient with no patient scored by a model
trained on them. Because the four models come from independent per-fold searches, each has its own
predicted-probability scale, and pooling across those scales does not behave like evaluating one
fixed model: pooled AUROC and AUPRC are systematically \textit{more conservative} than the fold-wise
mean, because ranking across incomparable scales degrades discrimination, while pooled calibration is
systematically \textit{more optimistic}, because folds that over- and under-predict partially cancel.
The pooled calibration curve therefore characterizes a four-model mixture that is never deployed as
such and should be read alongside the fold-wise mean rather than in place of it. Brier score and
decision curve analysis are unaffected, both being per-observation averages.

\subsection{Counterfactual generation}

This subsection gives the material needed to reproduce and audit the counterfactual stage of
Sections~\ref{sec:generalsetup} and~\ref{sec:instance-level-cf}: the exact DiCE instantiation used
for each fold model, the generation parameters applied to each selected instance, and the resulting
yield---first per fold model, then per individual patient. The yield tables also make explicit how
sparse the successful outcome was: of 61 misclassified or borderline candidates across the four
folds, only 8 produced any valid counterfactual, with one fold producing none at all. Those counts,
rather than the properties of the counterfactuals themselves, are the main quantitative caveat on the
counterfactual results, and are taken up in Sections~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}
and~\ref{sec:limitations}.
