\abstract{\textbf{Background:} Diabetic peripheral neuropathy is the most common polyneuropathy among Filipinos with diabetes but goes undiagnosed in up to 81\% of cases, as confirmation requires a costly and often geographically inaccessible nerve conduction study. We asked whether a model using only data a diabetes clinic can collect without electrodiagnostic equipment can prioritize patients for that referral, and whether counterfactual explanations can turn its predictions into actionable guidance.

\textbf{Methods:} Of 190 adults with diabetes recruited at a tertiary hospital in Quezon City, Philippines (2022--2023), 187 with complete data were labeled Confirmed (130) or Unconfirmed (57) neuropathy under Toronto consensus criteria; the nerve conduction study defining that label was excluded from every predictor set. Fourteen algorithms and twelve feature subsets, spanning clinical profile, comorbidities, the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument, bedside neurological examination, and Sudoscan, were benchmarked by ten repeats of stratified four-fold cross-validation on the area under the precision-recall curve. The selected model was tuned by nested Bayesian optimization, then interpreted through feature importance, Shapley attributions, and counterfactuals restricted to six clinically actionable features.

\textbf{Results:} Ensemble tree methods clustered indistinguishably at the top (best 0.892 versus a no-skill baseline of 0.695); CatBoost was carried forward for its fit to a small cohort and its smoother probability surface for counterfactual search. The tuned pipeline reached mean precision-recall area 0.886 (95\% CI 0.871--0.902) and sensitivity 0.886 (0.861--0.912); specificity was the unstable weak point (0.527, 0.483--0.571). Bedside neurological examination was the least redundant modality; Sudoscan plus examination alone came within 0.01 of the full feature set. Importance and Shapley analyses converged on decreased ankle reflex, diabetes duration, Sudoscan asymmetry, and decreased vibration sensation, all markers of established neuropathy rather than treatable targets. Counterfactuals, obtained for 8 of 61 candidate patients, changed glycated hemoglobin most often (91.1\%) but seldom alone, pairing it with insulin use (51.3\%), dyslipidemia (37.3\%), or hypertension (31.0\%).

\textbf{Conclusions:} Screening without electrodiagnostic input reached a sensitivity suitable for triage, limited by unstable specificity. Its strongest predictors measure established neuropathy; only counterfactual explanation surfaced the modifiable, multifactorial pathways a clinician can act on. This single-center proof of concept requires external and prospective validation.}

\section{Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{introduction}

% The Introduction section expands on the background of the work (some overlap with the Abstract is acceptable). The introduction should not include subheadings.


Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is the most common type of polyneuropathy affecting 42-58\% of Filipinos \cite{ropper2019adams, araneta2006hyperglycemia, dagang2016validation}.
Characterized by progressive nerve damage, DPN often presents with symptoms such as pain, numbness, and loss of sensation in the extremities, particularly the feet. Beyond its direct impact on quality of life, DPN is a major risk factor for foot ulcers, infections, and lower-limb amputations, contributing significantly to healthcare burden.

Despite being associated with reduced quality of life and debilitating complications,
DPN is underdiagnosed in up to 81\% of patients \cite{vinik2012neuropathy, ziegler2018painful}. Hurdles in the early diagnosis of DPN includes the insidious progression, inconsistent disease presentation and the need for complicated equipment for definitive diagnosis \cite{vinik2012neuropathy, ziegler2018painful, casellini2013sudoscan}.

Skin biopsy with quantitation of intraepidermal nerve fiber density is still the gold standard in detecting small fiber neuropathy, while sudorimetry is a rapidly evolving technology that can easily be integrated into clinical practice. The development of such technologies like the Sudoscan allows noninvasive and easy-to-perform tests for detection of small fiber neuropathy based on reverse iontophoresis and chronoamperometry to measure sudomotor function \cite{mayaudon:2010newsimple, vinik2015newage}.

Early detection and management of DPN are therefore critical, yet remain challenging due to the multifactorial nature of the disease and the heterogeneity of patient profiles. In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool for modeling complex, nonlinear relationships in medical data. By leveraging large clinical datasets, ML algorithms can identify patterns and interactions among risk factors that may not be readily apparent through traditional statistical approaches. 

Numerous studies have developed ML models with good discriminatory performance (AUCs ranging from 0.64 to 0.93), employing diverse algorithms such as random forest, XGBoost, and gradient boosting. Models often incorporate clinical, biochemical, and even traditional Chinese medicine features, enhancing predictive accuracy and relevance \cite{ma2025machine,jiang2024multifeature,cui2023study,lian2023Study,Wei2024construction}. Internal validation methods like cross-validation are widely used, and some studies report calibration and decision curve analyses, supporting clinical utility \cite{cui2023study}.

Despite the  predictive performance, many ML models---particularly ensemble and deep learning methods---are often criticized as ``black boxes,'' limiting their adoption in clinical settings where interpretability and trust are essential. To address this limitation, the field of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has gained increasing attention. XAI techniques aim to make model predictions more transparent by providing insights into how input features influence outcomes. Methods such as feature importance scores, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations), and partial dependence plots have been widely used to offer global and local interpretability. 

The application of XAI techniques such as SHAP, LIME, and Anchor explanations is increasingly common, providing transparent insights into feature importance and model decisions, which is critical for clinical trust and adoption \cite{wu2025prognostic,jiang2024multifeature, pandey2024decoding, wu2024interpretable, sheikh2025predicting}. Integration of XAI with multimodal data enables identification of key risk factors and supports personalized treatment recommendations \cite{nguyen2024interpretable, alHudaibi2025explainable}. Some frameworks incorporate human-in-the-loop feedback to enhance interpretability and clinical relevance \cite{lalwani2025generative}. While these approaches help elucidate model behavior, they primarily describe associations rather than actionable insights, which are crucial for clinical decision-making.

Counterfactual explanations represent a more recent and clinically meaningful approach within XAI. Instead of merely explaining why a prediction was made, counterfactuals answer ``what-if'' questions by identifying minimal changes to a patient's features that would lead to a different predicted outcome. For example, a counterfactual explanation might suggest how modifying certain clinical variables---such as glycemic control or body mass index---could reduce a patient's predicted risk of developing DPN. This aligns closely with the needs of personalized medicine, where interventions are tailored to individual patients based on modifiable risk factors. Several methodological approaches for generating counterfactual explanations have been proposed in the literature. One of the most popular is the DiCE framework \cite{mothilal2020explaining}, an optimization-based method, which formulate counterfactual generation as a constrained optimization problem that seeks minimal changes to input features while achieving a desired prediction;

The integration of machine learning and counterfactual explanations offers a promising framework for both the prediction and interpretation of diabetic peripheral neuropathy. By combining high predictive accuracy with actionable, patient-specific insights, this approach has the potential to support clinicians in early diagnosis, risk assessment, and treatment planning. Moreover, it bridges the gap between advanced computational models and practical clinical utility, fostering greater trust and adoption of AI-driven tools in healthcare. 

This study aims to investigate the application of machine learning models in conjunction with counterfactual explanation techniques for the prediction and interpretation of diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) risk, thereby contributing to the growing body of research on interpretable and actionable artificial intelligence in medicine. The study also examines the potential insights derived from incorporating Sudoscan measurements---a modality that is comparatively underrepresented in many AI-based DPN studies, which often rely primarily on routine clinical and demographic variables---while deliberately excluding nerve conduction study results from the predictor set, since these constitute the diagnostic reference standard that the model is intended to triage patients toward. Furthermore, by utilizing data collected within the Philippine clinical context, this work enhances the geographic diversity of current studies and provides context-specific insights into the application of explainable AI for DPN.

\section{Data Collection}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\label{datacollection}

A total of 201 adult Filipino patients diagnosed with Diabetes Mellitus (DM) were recruited
between 2022 and 2023. Subjects with limb amputation, gangrenous/ necrotic foot, with implanted
cardiac pacemakers or defibrillators, and patients with other known causes of polyneuropathy were
excluded. Remaining 190 qualified subjects underwent a series of detailed assessment comprising of
Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI) test, serial neurologic tests, and lower extremity NCV
examination to detect the presence of DPN. Subjects were then classified based on the 2009 Toronto
consensus criteria as confirmed, probable, possible, or no DPN \cite{tesfaye2010diabetic}.  Of the 190 enrolled patients, 3 patients were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete data, resulting in a final dataset of 187 patients.  As the purpose of the study is prescreening for DPN, the study focused on the binary classification of patients into two groups: Confirmed DPN and Unconfirmed DPN. This resulted in a dataset of 130 patients with Confirmed DPN and 57 patients with Unconfirmed DPN.  The patient flow and classification breakdown is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:patient_flow_classification}.

Neuropathic symptoms were documented by subjectively asking patients for presence of any
distal extremity numbness, paresthesia, or pain, and by asking them to answer the Filipino validated
version of MNSI \cite{dagang2016validation}. Abnormal neurologic signs were detected using the standard 10g Semmes Weinstein monofilament examination (SWME) and standard 128-Hz clanging tuning fork tests \cite{feng2009semmes,oyer2007quantitative}. Additional examination for pinprick sensation was done using the same criteria for SWME. Achilles tendon reflex testing was also done and dichotomized subjects as either normal or hypo/areflexive. All neurological examinations were conducted by a single trained physician.

Nerve conduction studies were performed at a stable skin temperature of 32°C and a room
temperature of 24°C using Nihon Kohen EMG equipment with a filter width of 20 Hz to 3 kHz, amplifier
setting at 200 $\mu$V/div, and sweep speed of 10 ms/div. 

The following nerve attributes were measured: 
\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0em 
\item Sural sensory nerve action potentials (SNAP) and conduction velocities (CV)
\item Superficial peroneal SNAP and CV
\item Tibial motor nerve compound muscle action potentials (CMAP), CV, distal latencies (DL) and
F-wave latencies
\end{itemize}

All NCV testing was done by a single trained technician on the same day as other
neurological examinations. Finally, sudomotor testing using Sudoscan was performed on each participant. Subjects placed palms of their hands and the soles of their feet on stainless steel electrodes and an incremental low direct voltage (<4V) was applied for about 2 minutes. The device produced an average electrochemical skin conductance (ESC) result for both hands and feet which is based on stimulation of sweat glands by the low-level voltage, allowing evidence of sweat dysfunction \cite{garcia2022detection}.

The full list of features, their groupings, description, and code are shown in Table~\ref{tab:clinical_data}.

This study was approved by the local Institutional Ethics Review Board and all subjects gave their written consent prior to participating.

\subsection{Machine Learning Modeling}
%*************************************
In this section, we describe the machine learning models used, the model and feature-set selection procedure, the safeguards against data leakage and against the effects of a small, imbalanced sample, and the nested-cross-validation hyperparameter optimization and explainability procedures applied to the selected model.

\subsubsection{Models}
%=====================
\label{sec:models}

A diverse set of machine learning algorithms was evaluated in this study using default or standard parameter settings to ensure a consistent and unbiased baseline comparison. These models can be broadly grouped according to their underlying learning paradigms:

\textit{(a) Linear and Probabilistic Models}.
This group includes Logistic Regression, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), Naive Bayes (Gaussian), and Linear Support Vector Machine (Linear SVM). These models are generally characterized by strong interpretability and computational efficiency. Logistic Regression and Linear SVM are effective in high-dimensional spaces and provide robust linear decision boundaries. LDA and QDA model class-conditional distributions, with QDA allowing nonlinear decision boundaries through class-specific covariance matrices. Naive Bayes is particularly efficient and performs well under conditional independence assumptions, while the SGD classifier enables scalable optimization for large datasets.

\textit{(b) Distance-Based and Instance-Based Learning}.
The k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) algorithm falls under this category. It is a non-parametric method that makes predictions based on the proximity of data points in feature space. Its strength lies in its simplicity and ability to capture complex decision boundaries without explicit model training, although it may be sensitive to feature scaling and noise.

\textit{(c) Tree-Based and Ensemble Methods}.
This group includes Decision Tree, Random Forest, Extra Trees, and Gradient Boosting. Decision Trees are intuitive and capable of capturing nonlinear relationships and feature interactions. Random Forest and Extra Trees extend this capability through ensemble learning, improving generalization and reducing variance via bagging and randomized feature selection. Gradient Boosting builds models sequentially to correct previous errors, often achieving strong predictive performance.

\textit{(d) Advanced Gradient Boosting Frameworks}.
More sophisticated implementations of boosting are represented by XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. These algorithms are optimized for efficiency and performance on structured/tabular data. XGBoost incorporates regularization and efficient handling of sparse data; LightGBM employs histogram-based learning and leaf-wise tree growth for speed and scalability; CatBoost is particularly effective in handling categorical variables and mitigating overfitting through ordered boosting techniques.

\textit{(e) Kernel-Based Models}.
The RBF Support Vector Machine (RBF SVM) extends linear SVM by applying a radial basis function kernel, enabling the modeling of nonlinear decision boundaries. This makes it powerful for complex datasets where linear separability is not sufficient.

\textit{(f) Baseline Model}.
A Naive classifier that predicts the majority class was included as a baseline to contextualize the performance of all other models.

Overall, this diverse selection of models allows for a comprehensive evaluation across linear, nonlinear, probabilistic, and ensemble-based learning paradigms, each with distinct strengths in terms of interpretability, flexibility, robustness, and predictive power.

\subsubsection{Model and Feature Set Selection}
%==============================================
\label{sec:modelselection}

Given the relatively limited number of unconfirmed cases in the dataset, a robust evaluation strategy was used to generate reliable performance estimates for the model-selection step. Fourteen algorithms (Section~\ref{sec:models}) were benchmarked with default/standard hyperparameters under a repeated stratified k-fold cross-validation scheme---4 folds $\times$ 10 repetitions, for 40 validation runs per model---against each of twelve feature sets (Table~\ref{tab:feature_sets}), giving $14 \times 12 \times 40$ scored runs in total. Stratification ensures that each fold preserves the class distribution of the dataset, which is particularly important given 57 Unconfirmed patients; repetition reduces the variance of the resulting performance estimate by averaging results across multiple random partitions.

To assess the contribution of different feature subsets, all fourteen models were first trained on the complete feature set to establish baseline performance, then retrained on eleven further feature sets that systematically remove or isolate each feature group defined in Table~\ref{tab:clinical_data}-A (Table~\ref{tab:feature_sets}). Moreover, additive sets anchored on Sudoscan were additionally constructed given Sudoscan's established role in peripheral neuropathy screening, allowing assessment of its complementary value when paired with other feature
groups. This ablation strategy identifies which feature groups contribute most to predictive performance and quantifies the value of the Sudoscan.

For every model--feature-set combination, the 40 per-run scores of each metric were summarized by their mean; models and feature sets were then ranked by mean Area Under the Precision--Recall Curve, since AUPRC directly summarizes the precision--recall trade-off that defines the intended screening use (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). CatBoost on the full feature set attained the highest mean AUPRC and was carried forward to hyperparameter optimization (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}); the full model $\times$ feature-set comparison (mean AUPRC across all fourteen models and twelve feature sets) and the run-to-run distribution of AUPRC for the selected model and feature set are reported in the Discussion.

In addition, multicollinearity among the 22 candidate predictors was addressed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF): features exceeding a VIF of 5 were dropped to construct the reduced ``NoCol'' feature set (Table~\ref{tab:feature_sets}), aimed at assessing model stability and interpretability under reduced collinearity.

\subsubsection{Performance Metrics}
%==================================
\label{sec:metrics}
For each of the 40 validation runs, eight metrics were recorded: accuracy, precision, sensitivity (recall), specificity, the Youden index, F1, ROC-AUC, and AUPRC. Sensitivity and specificity jointly express the clinical trade-off of a pre-diagnostic screening tool, while AUPRC---the metric used to rank models and feature sets (Section~\ref{sec:modelselection})---was prioritized over ROC-AUC because its two axes are the quantities that govern the intended use of the model. With Confirmed DPN as the positive class, recall is the fraction of confirmed patients the screen refers onward for nerve conduction study, and precision is the positive predictive value of that referral---the fraction of referrals to a resource-intensive and often geographically inaccessible test that prove justified. AUPRC summarizes this trade-off across all possible decision thresholds and is anchored to the cohort's Confirmed prevalence of 0.695, the no-skill baseline against which the reported values should be read. ROC-AUC instead plots recall against the false-positive rate, a quantity that is not acted upon at the point of referral, and is by construction invariant to prevalence.

\subsubsection{Preventing Data Leakage}
%======================================
\label{sec:leakage}

Every stage of the pipeline enforces a strict separation between the data used to fit or tune a model and the data used to evaluate it, so that reported performance is not optimistically biased:

\begin{itemize}
    \itemsep0em
    \item \textit{Predictor set.} The nerve conduction study variables (Table~\ref{tab:clinical_data}-B) are excluded from every modeling stage, before selection begins. These measurements are the diagnostic reference standard from which the Confirmed/Unconfirmed label is derived under the 2009 Toronto consensus criteria, so admitting them as predictors would leak the outcome into the feature set; their exclusion is also what makes the model usable for its intended purpose, pre-diagnostic screening where NCS is unavailable. The 22 candidate predictors of Table~\ref{tab:clinical_data}-A are the only variables any model in this study sees.
    \item \textit{Selection.} The 40 repeated-CV runs for each model--feature-set pair each fit on a training fold and score on a disjoint test fold; no test fold is ever seen during fitting.
    \item \textit{Hyperparameter optimization.} The nested cross-validation described in Section~\ref{sec:hpo} confines hyperparameter search, refitting, and decision-threshold selection entirely to each split's training set; the corresponding test set is touched exactly once, after tuning and thresholding are complete, purely to record performance. 
    \item \textit{Explainability and counterfactual generation.} Both stages reuse the training-set models and split-specific thresholds already fitted and selected during optimization (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}); they never refit against, or select a threshold from, a test set.
\end{itemize}

This separation is what licenses treating test-set performance, and the diagnostics computed downstream of it---discrimination, calibration, and the SHAP attributions of Section~\ref{sec:explainability}---as out-of-sample estimates rather than measurements of how well the model fits data it has already seen. CatBoost's feature importances are the one exception: they are read off the fitted trees rather than from held-out predictions, and so describe each split's training set by construction.

\subsubsection{Handling Class Imbalance and a Small Sample Size}
%===============================================================
\label{sec:classimbalance}

The analyzed cohort is small by machine-learning standards (187 patients, 22 candidate predictors) and imbalanced (130 Confirmed vs.\ 57 Unconfirmed, 69.5\%/30.5\%). Two complementary strategies address this, and a third common strategy---synthetic minority oversampling---was deliberately not used.

Repeated stratified cross-validation (Sections~\ref{sec:modelselection} and~\ref{sec:hpo}) is the primary defense against the variance a small $n$ introduces into any single performance estimate: stratification constrains every fold to reproduce the cohort's class distribution as closely as the fold sizes allow, and repetition (10 repeats at the selection stage, 10 at the optimization stage) averages out the sampling variability of any one particular partition of 187 patients into folds.

Class imbalance itself is handled inside the model rather than by resampling the data. CatBoost's positive-class weight (\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight})-- which scales the loss contribution of positive-class examples---was tuned per train/test split over the range 0.3--1.0, alongside tree depth, learning rate, and $L_2$ leaf regularization (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}). The Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was deliberately not used: with only 57 Unconfirmed patients across 22 features, SMOTE's nearest-neighbor interpolation would synthesize clinically fabricated patients in a feature space that is already sparse. Reweighting the loss using the positive-class weight keeps every training example a real patient, something that matters for the grounding of the downstream task of  generating counterfactuals. 

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Optimization}
%==========================================
\label{sec:hpo}

CatBoost, selected on the full feature set as described in Section~\ref{sec:modelselection}, was carried forward to nested cross-validation hyperparameter optimization (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview}, upper panels). An outer repeated stratified $k$-fold partitioning---$k=4$ folds $\times$ 10 repeats, under a fixed random seed---divides the cohort into 40 \textit{train/test splits}, over which final performance is reported; nested inside every one of those 40 splits, a second, inner stratified $k$-fold partitioning ($k=3$) subdivides that split's \textit{training set} alone into three \textit{tuning folds}, used exclusively for hyperparameter search. These two levels serve different purposes and operate on different data, which is the mechanism described in Section~\ref{sec:leakage}.

Within each train/test split, an Optuna Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler ran 100 trials, each fitting three CatBoost models---one per tuning fold---and scoring the trial by the mean AUPRC across the three held-out tuning folds. Four hyperparameters were searched: tree depth (4--10, integer), learning rate (0.003--0.3, log-uniform), $L_2$ leaf regularization (1.0--10.0, uniform), and the positive-class weight (0.3--1.0, uniform; Section~\ref{sec:classimbalance}). All other CatBoost settings were held constant across every trial, fold, and repeat: a logistic loss function, AUC as the evaluation metric, a maximum of 500 boosting iterations, and early stopping after 50 rounds without improvement.

After the search, the winning trial's parameters were refit once on the full training set, and separately used to produce three further out-of-fold (OOF) refits over the same three tuning folds, yielding one held-out probability for every training-set patient. A classification threshold was then selected, per train/test split, as the value maximizing the F1 score on the precision--recall curve of those OOF probabilities---never on the test set. The refit model and its selected threshold were then applied once to the untouched test set, recording the threshold and eight metrics in Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}: the threshold itself, accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1, the Youden index, ROC-AUC, and AUPRC. These per-split values are pooled across all 40 train/test splits as a mean $\pm$ 95\% confidence interval (normal approximation, $z=1.96$, computed excluding any iteration where a metric is undefined for that fold), which is what is reported in Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats} in the Discussion.

\textit{One modeling pipeline, evaluated by cross-validation.} It bears stating explicitly, because it is easy to misread otherwise: the procedure above is \textit{one} modeling pipeline---one algorithm (CatBoost), one hyperparameter-search procedure---evaluated by 4-fold cross-validation repeated 10 times, not four, or forty, separate studies. Its primary performance claim is the single pooled mean $\pm$ 95\% CI over the 40 train/test splits described above. The models and splits of the \textit{first} of those 10 repeats are additionally saved (its four fold models, their training/test partitions, tuned hyperparameters, and thresholds) for reuse, without refitting, by the Explainability and Counterfactual Generation stages below. Consequently, when those later sections refer to ``the four models'' or ``model~0'' through ``model~3,'' this denotes the four fold models of that single saved repeat of the one nested-CV procedure above---not four independently designed experiments, and not a claim that any one of the four is individually authoritative. Their agreement or disagreement (e.g., in feature importance or SHAP ranking) is itself evidence about the stability of the one pipeline, not a comparison between competing pipelines.

\textit{Reuse of the saved models.} The counterfactual generation stage uses those four saved models directly, without refitting. The diagnostics and explainability analyses refit each of them first, so that attributions can be reported against named clinical variables rather than against anonymous input positions. Each model is refitted on its own saved training set, under that fold's tuned hyperparameters---the fold-specific configuration selected above, not a single shared one---and its original random seed, so the refit reproduces the saved model exactly: its predictions are identical to it, not those of a second, slightly different fit.

\subsubsection{Pooled Model Diagnostics}
%======================================
\label{sec:pooleddiagnostics}

Two complementary views of the four fold models' out-of-sample performance are reported. \textit{Per-fold} (``all splits'') ROC and precision--recall curves are plotted separately for each of the four fold models, to show whether discrimination is stable across folds rather than an artefact of one particular training partition; the fold-wise feature importance and SHAP attributions serve the same purpose and are described in Section~\ref{sec:explainability}. \textit{Pooled} diagnostics---AUROC, AUPRC, calibration (reliability), and decision curve analysis---are computed by concatenating the four fold models' predictions on their own respective test sets, giving one out-of-sample prediction per patient across the full 187-patient cohort, no patient scored by a model trained on them. This is the only point in the study at which the four folds are combined into a single curve, and it carries a cost set out next.

Because the four fold models come from independent per-fold hyperparameter searches (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}), each has its own predicted-probability scale (the tuned positive-class weight, for instance, differs by fold), and pooling raw scores across those four scales does not behave like evaluating one fixed model. This has a specific, documented, and opposite-signed effect on the two families of pooled diagnostics, which the Discussion presents but does not re-derive: pooled AUROC and AUPRC are systematically \textit{more conservative} than the fold-wise mean, because ranking probabilities across incomparable scales degrades discrimination; pooled calibration is systematically \textit{more optimistic} than the fold-wise mean ECE, because folds that over-predict and folds that under-predict partially cancel once pooled. The pooled calibration curve therefore characterizes a four-model mixture that is never deployed as such, and should be read alongside the fold-wise mean $\pm$ std rather than in place of it; Brier score and decision curve analysis are unaffected, both being per-observation averages---pooled net benefit at any threshold is exactly the size-weighted mean of the fold net benefits at that threshold.

\subsection{Explainability Analysis}
%*************************************
\label{sec:explainability}

Post-hoc explainability is computed on the same four retrained fold models (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}) but, unlike the pooled diagnostics above, is reported fold by fold and never averaged into one ranking: four separately grown tree ensembles, each under its own tuned configuration, admit no meaningful mean. What the four folds agree on---which features occupy the upper tier, rather than their exact order within it---is the stability evidence being claimed.

\textit{CatBoost feature importance.} For each fold model, importances are taken from CatBoost's built-in importance measure under its default PredictionValuesChange criterion, which scores a feature by how much the ensemble's output shifts, on average, as that feature's split conditions change; scores are normalized to sum to 100 across the 22 non-NCS features. The quantity is read off the fitted trees themselves, so it describes what the model built from its own training set, with no test data entering the computation.

\textit{SHAP attributions.} SHAP values are computed for each fold model on that fold's held-out test set only. The explainer wraps the model's positive-class probability output as a black-box function rather than exploiting CatBoost's internal tree paths, and uses the same test fold as its background distribution; attributions are therefore expressed in units of predicted probability and are relative to that fold's mean test-set prediction. For each feature, the mean absolute SHAP value across the fold's test patients is saved, summarizing influence magnitude irrespective of sign. The two methods are consequently comparable in ranking but not in value: importance scores are order $10$ on a scale summing to 100, SHAP magnitudes order $10^{-2}$ on the probability scale.

Both are reported as feature $\times$ model heatmaps, rows ordered by the cross-fold mean and color-coded by clinical domain (Figures~\ref{fig:feature_importances} and \ref{fig:shap}). They answer complementary questions---what the fitted ensemble relies on, against how much each feature moves the predicted probability for patients it has not seen---but neither answers whether a feature can be acted upon, which is what the counterfactual generation stage below is for.

\subsection{Counterfactual Generation}
%*************************************
To provide interpretable insights into the predictions of the trained machine learning models, counterfactual explanations were generated using the DiCE (Diverse Counterfactual Explanations) framework \cite{mothilal2020explaining}. DiCE is well-suited for this study due to its model-agnostic capability, enabling its application across a wide range of classifiers, including complex ensemble models. Furthermore, it supports diversity-aware counterfactual generation, allowing the identification of multiple plausible feature modifications that can alter model predictions---an important requirement in clinical decision support. Its ability to incorporate feature constraints (e.g., permitted ranges and immutable variables) ensures that generated counterfactuals remain realistic and clinically meaningful.

Only Suitable and Conditional features were included in counterfactual generation to ensure that all generated counterfactual scenarios correspond to clinically meaningful and potentially achievable patient states.}

\subsubsection{General Setup}
%============================
\label{sec:generalsetup}
Counterfactuals are generated separately for each of the four saved fold models (Section~\ref{sec:hpo}), each wrapped with \textit{that fold's own} tuned decision threshold rather than a threshold shared across folds---consistent with Section~\ref{sec:hpo}'s point that these are four fold-specific configurations of one pipeline, not four independent choices. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline_overview} (lower panel) places the procedure described in this subsection within the overall pipeline.

\textit{Global Permitted Range}.
To ensure feasibility of generated counterfactuals, a global permitted range was defined for all continuous features, using every patient in the dataset (training and test partitions of the fold together, so the range describes the whole observed cohort rather than one fold's split of it) The bounds were computed as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \itemsep0em
    \item Minimum: $\max(0,\ x_{min} - \sigma)$
    \item Maximum: $x_{max} + \sigma$
\end{itemize}
where $x_{min}$ and $x_{max}$ denote the observed minimum and maximum values, respectively, of each feature over the whole cohort, and $\sigma$ is its standard deviation, also over the whole cohort. Bounding the minimum at 0 keeps a naturally non-negative clinical quantity (e.g., HbA1c) from being pushed below zero by the $-\sigma$ margin; the $+\sigma$ margin at both ends allows a counterfactual to propose a value a little beyond the observed range while remaining plausible.

\textit{DiCE Configuration Parameters}. The configuration of the DiCE framework is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dice_setup}. The genetic algorithm was selected as the optimization method because of the presence of multiple categorical features which renders the use of gradients for optimization not feasible. Genetic algorithm is also effectiveness in exploring high-dimensional feature spaces and generating diverse counterfactuals under multiple constraints.

\subsubsection{Local Counterfactual Analysis}
%============================================
We describe here how instances were chosen for counterfactual generation and how the permitted range was computed for each instance.

\textit{Targeted Instance Selection}.
Within each fold's test set, local counterfactual explanations were generated for misclassified and borderline cases. Borderline cases are instances whose predicted probability lies within $\delta = 0.08$ of \textit{that fold's own} tuned decision threshold---not a single threshold shared across folds. These instances represent cases where a small feature change may alter the predicted outcome, making them particularly informative for analysis; Figure~\ref{fig:univariable_discrimination}'s discrimination scale gives a sense of why so many patients fall this close to the boundary given the modest signal in most individual features.

\textit{Local Permitted Range}.
For each selected instance, an instance-specific permitted range was defined for each feature to vary. Similar to the global permitted range, this ensures that generated counterfactuals remain both realistic and tailored to the specific instance:

\begin{itemize}
    \itemsep0em
    \item Minimum: $\max(0,\ \mathrm{instance\_value} - \sigma)$
    \item Maximum: $\mathrm{instance\_value} + \sigma$
\end{itemize}
where, as for the global range, $\sigma$ is each feature's standard deviation over the whole cohort; the interval is centered on the patient's own observed value rather than the cohort mean, so a proposed change is scaled to a magnitude already seen in the cohort but anchored to where this particular patient starts from.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Discussions should be brief and focused. 

In this section, we discuss the performance of the machine learning models evaluated for pre-diagnostic DPN screening, the rationale for selecting CatBoost over closely competing alternatives, and the hyperparameter optimization and local explainability of its predictions through feature importance and SHAP. We then turn to counterfactual explanation analysis---the central contribution of this study---describing the DiCE configuration used to generate patient-level counterfactuals, presenting detailed case studies, and synthesizing the aggregate feature-change patterns across all candidate instances. We close by outlining how this pipeline could be used by clinicians in practice, and by situating these results within a decision-support framing rather than as a deployment-ready diagnostic tool.

To be explicit about scope, this study is a proof-of-concept investigation into whether counterfactual explanations can make a machine-learning-based DPN screening model actionable for clinicians, not a validated or deployment-ready clinical instrument. To our knowledge, it is among the first studies to systematically integrate counterfactual explanations into a pre-diagnostic DPN screening pipeline intended for low-resource clinical settings where nerve conduction studies are costly or geographically inaccessible; the counterfactual analysis, rather than the underlying classifier's raw discriminative performance, is the primary contribution of this work.

\subsection{Feature Set and Model Selection}
%*******************************************

\subsubsection{Baseline Model Evaluation: Rationale and Metric Selection}
%========================================================================
The primary objective of this baseline evaluation was to identify the most promising classification algorithm for subsequent optimization as a machine learning-based pre-diagnostic screening tool for Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy (DPN). Fourteen algorithms spanning diverse learning paradigms, together with a naive majority-class baseline, were assessed using default hyperparameters and repeated stratified k-fold cross-validation (10 repeats × 4 folds; 40 total runs) to obtain stable, low-variance performance estimates without data leakage.
Given the inherent class imbalance in this dataset---130 DPN-confirmed patients versus 57 unconfirmed cases (69.5\%/30.5\%)---AUPRC was chosen as the primary ranking metric in preference to ROC-AUC. This decision is well-supported in the methodological literature: ROC-AUC can present an overly optimistic picture of classifier performance under class imbalance because it incorporates the true negative rate, which inflates when the negative class is underrepresented. AUPRC, by contrast, focuses exclusively on the precision-recall trade-off and is therefore more sensitive to performance differences among classifiers on the minority class \cite{saito2015precision, davis2006relationship}. The naive baseline---which predicts the majority class uniformly---achieved an AUPRC of 0.695 (essentially the empirical prevalence) across all feature sets and conditions, despite attaining perfect sensitivity (1.0) and an accuracy of 69.5\%, illustrating precisely why accuracy and sensitivity alone are insufficient evaluation criteria in imbalanced screening tasks.

\subsubsection{Model Performance and Selection}
%==============================================
Across all 40 runs on the full feature set, ensemble tree-based methods consistently outperformed other model families (Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics}). Random Forest attained the highest mean AUPRC (0.892 $\pm$ 0.039), narrowly ahead of CatBoost (0.889 $\pm$ 0.044) and Extra Trees (0.885 $\pm$ 0.042); the three are statistically indistinguishable, with all three means falling well within one standard deviation of one another. Naive Bayes (0.884), Logistic Regression (0.880), RBF SVM (0.878), and LDA (0.878) followed closely behind, underscoring that the full feature set carries strong, model-agnostic signal for DPN status rather than favoring any single learning paradigm.

From a clinical deployment standpoint, the balance between sensitivity and specificity is of critical importance in pre-diagnostic screening. A tool intended to triage patients toward the more costly and less accessible Nerve Conduction Study (NCS) must prioritize minimizing false negatives (i.e., maintaining high sensitivity) to avoid denying care to patients who truly have DPN.

Under this clinical framing, Random Forest and CatBoost occupy essentially the same operating point: sensitivity of 0.916 and 0.913, and specificity of 0.529 and 0.512, respectively. RBF SVM achieved the highest sensitivity overall (0.938), but at the cost of the lowest specificity among the top models (0.453). Because Random Forest's raw AUPRC edge over CatBoost here is well within sampling noise, the choice between the two---carried forward through the remainder of this pipeline---cannot rest on this table alone; Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf} below makes the case for CatBoost on grounds that matter downstream rather than on this baseline ranking.

\subsubsection{Feature Set Analysis}
%===================================
The heatmap of average AUPRC across feature sets (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_heatmaps}) reveals several findings with direct clinical relevance. First, the top models (Random Forest, CatBoost, Extra Trees) are remarkably robust to the removal of most feature groups: ablating Clinical Profile (NoProf), Comorbidities (NoCom), or MNSI (NoMsi) produced negligible or no AUPRC change for these models (all within 0.01 of the full-feature-set value). This robustness implies that no single feature group is singularly indispensable, and that redundancy across the feature space is substantial. The exception is Neurological Examination features (NoNeuro), whose removal produced the largest drop observed for any ablation among the top models (Random Forest and CatBoost both fall to 0.86), suggesting these bedside signs are the least redundant predictors in the set.

The Sudoscan-only feature set (Sudo) understandably lowered performance for every model (Random Forest 0.82, CatBoost 0.83), while the additive Sudoscan-anchored feature sets (SudoProf, SudoCom, SudoNeuro, SudoMnsi) demonstrated modest but inconsistent improvements over Sudoscan-only, suggesting diminishing returns from adding other feature groups once Sudoscan data are available. One exception worth noting is that the SudoNeuro combination (Sudoscan with Neurological Examination findings) recovered to within 0.01 of full-feature-set performance for both top models (0.89 Random Forest, 0.90 CatBoost), indicating that simple bedside neurological signs---vibration sensation, pinprick, light touch, and ankle reflex---may complement electrochemical skin conductance data in a synergistic manner that benefits less flexible classifiers, and that a reduced Sudoscan+neuro-exam panel could be a viable lower-burden alternative to the full feature set in settings where Sudoscan is available but a complete profile, comorbidity, and MNSI workup is not practical.

We note that since these metrics are based on default hyperparameters, a more complete picture of feature importance will be explored during the hyperparameter optimization and explainability analysis that follow.

\subsubsection{Reproducibility and Stability of Estimates}
%=========================================================
The use of 10-repeat × 4-fold stratified cross-validation was chosen deliberately to obtain stable performance estimates given the modest sample size (n = 187). With a single k-fold split, estimates can exhibit high variance due to the stochastic partitioning of a small dataset \cite{kim2009estimating}.

Repeated cross-validation substantially reduces this variance, and the violin plot distributions (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_violins}) confirm that the top models maintain elevated, largely overlapping AUPRC distributions. Random Forest shows the highest median and a comparatively tight interquartile range; CatBoost's median and box are close behind, but with a longer lower tail reaching toward 0.75, indicating an occasional weaker fold rather than a systematically less stable ranking. This is a relevant nuance for the CatBoost-versus-Random-Forest discussion below: the two are close on central tendency but CatBoost is marginally more variable at this stage. The absence of hyperparameter tuning or threshold optimization in this phase is also intentional: these results represent genuine baseline performance, ensuring that the subsequent model selection and optimization pipeline is not confounded by information leakage from the full dataset.

\subsubsection{Study Limitations at the Baseline Evaluation Stage}
%=================================================================
Several limitations must be acknowledged at this stage. The dataset is relatively small (n = 187), and though repeated stratified cross-validation mitigates instability, external validation on an independent cohort remains necessary before clinical deployment. The class imbalance, while modest compared to many medical screening contexts, may still influence threshold-dependent metrics (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy) reported here, all of which used the default 0.5 probability threshold---a suboptimal choice for screening applications. Threshold calibration and optimization are addressed in the subsequent modeling phase.

\subsubsection{Selected Feature Set and Model}
%=============================================
Despite Random Forest's narrow edge in raw AUPRC, CatBoost was selected as the model to carry forward into hyperparameter optimization and counterfactual generation, on the strength of a comparable sensitivity/specificity operating point at this stage combined with the methodological considerations detailed in Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf}. The full feature set was retained as the training data for all subsequent stages, since it consistently achieved high performance across multiple methods and the feature-set ablations discussed above showed no clinically compelling reason to discard any feature group at this point in the pipeline.

\subsubsection{CatBoost versus Random Forest: Methodological Justification}
%==========================================================================
\label{sec:catboost_vs_rf}
Because Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics} does not, by itself, favor CatBoost over Random Forest, the justification for carrying CatBoost---rather than Random Forest---through hyperparameter optimization, explainability analysis, and counterfactual generation rests on two considerations that this baseline table cannot capture.

First, small-sample regularization. With only 187 patients (approximately 140 per training set), CatBoost's ordered boosting computes each tree's residuals using a permutation-restricted subset that excludes the point being scored, which is specifically designed to guard against the target leakage that plain gradient boosting is prone to when the same observations repeatedly inform both split statistics and gradients. Random Forest's bagged, full-depth CART trees are a different and less structurally constrained estimator at this sample size: individual trees can produce single- or few-sample leaves that bagging only partially averages away. CatBoost's symmetric (oblivious) tree structure, in which every node at a given depth shares the same split condition, further restricts the hypothesis space in a way that is particularly suited to small $n$. CatBoost's native handling of categorical comorbidity flags, and its class reweighting in place of synthetic oversampling (Methods, Section~\ref{sec:leakage}), also avoid the one-hot dimensionality inflation and synthetic-patient fabrication risk that would otherwise compound on a cohort this small \cite{dorogush2018catboost}.

Second, and more decisively for this study's aims, compatibility with the counterfactual-generation pipeline. The DiCE genetic search used to produce every counterfactual in Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual} treats the model as a black box and drives its fitness function directly from the model's predicted class probability. Random Forest's predicted probability is a vote-share average assembled from leaf-class proportions across the ensemble; on a small, high-purity dataset, many leaves become pure or near-pure, producing flat regions of the probability surface with little gradient signal for a genetic search to climb. CatBoost's output, by contrast, is an additive sum of many shallow trees at a small learning rate passed through a sigmoid---a smoother, higher-resolution score whose incremental changes translate into smoother, more monotonic probability movement. This property is exactly what the ``minimal, sufficient change'' logic behind a clinically usable counterfactual depends on, and it is the reason a coarser Random Forest probability surface would be expected to widen, not narrow, the flat regions where DiCE's genetic search already struggles (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility} below).

In short, the two models are statistically indistinguishable on this baseline table, so the deciding factors are properties that matter specifically for a 187-patient cohort and for a counterfactual-explanation pipeline built on the model's predicted-probability surface---not a leaderboard win for either model.

\subsection{CatBoost Model Training and Optimization}
%****************************************************
This subsection discusses the results of the nested cross-validation hyperparameter optimization procedure for CatBoost on the full feature set, whose design---the 4-fold $\times$ 10-repeat train/test scheme, the 3-fold Optuna TPE search run inside each training set, and the out-of-fold threshold selection that keeps each test set untouched until it is scored exactly once---is described in full in Section~\ref{sec:hpo}. We first examine CatBoost's overall performance across the pipeline's 4-fold structure using the full repeated cross-validation scheme (Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}, pooling all 40 train/test splits), then turn to the four individually-tuned fold models from the first of the ten repeats (Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}) that are saved for reuse, without refitting, by the Explainability and Counterfactual Generation analyses below.

\subsubsection{CatBoost Repeated Cross-Validation}
%=================================================
Before examining the four individually-tuned fold models used throughout the remainder of this study (Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}, next), we first establish CatBoost's overall performance across the pipeline's 4-fold structure at the level of the whole repeated cross-validation scheme: 10 independent repetitions of the 4-fold split, yielding 40 total evaluation runs, with aggregate estimates presented in Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}. This overall view is the appropriate starting point precisely because any single partitioning of a modest-sized dataset carries some risk of being unrepresentative of the underlying data distribution \cite{kim2009estimating}; establishing that the pipeline's performance is stable across 40 independent partitions, before selecting any one specific set of fold models to analyze in detail, avoids drawing conclusions from what could otherwise be a fold-specific artifact. As with the selection stage (Section~\ref{sec:models}), these 40 runs represent one modeling pipeline evaluated repeatedly, not 40 separately trained studies.

The mean AUPRC across all 40 runs was 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.871--0.902, STD = 0.049), with a confidence interval spanning just 3.1 percentage points, indicating that model performance is highly reproducible across varying data splits, an important criterion for clinical tool reliability \cite{steyerberg2010assessing}.

The mean sensitivity of 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.861--0.912)---the most clinically critical metric for a pre-diagnostic screening instrument---remained high and reasonably well-constrained, with an 95\% CI spanning roughly 5 percentage points and a STD of 0.083. This indicates that the model's ability to correctly identify DPN-positive patients is comparatively robust to partition variability, a finding of direct clinical relevance: in a screening context where the consequence of a missed DPN case includes delayed intervention, progressive nerve damage, and increased risk of foot ulceration or lower-limb amputation, a consistently high sensitivity is more valuable than a high average sensitivity with wide variability \cite{tesfaye2010}. By contrast, specificity showed the greatest instability across runs (mean = 0.527, STD = 0.141, 95\% CI: 0.483--0.571), confirming that the model's ability to correctly exclude DPN-negative patients was more sensitive to partition composition. This reflects the relatively smaller size of the Unconfirmed-DPN class (n = 57) and, as the per-fold analysis below shows, fold-by-fold variability in both Bayesian threshold selection and tuned hyperparameters.

The mean F1-score of 0.845 (95\% CI: 0.831--0.860) summarizes the balance between precision and sensitivity in a single number, and its reasonably narrow confidence interval indicates this balance is stable across partitions. Because F1 weights precision and sensitivity equally, it should be read alongside sensitivity and specificity above rather than in isolation: for this screening use case, where minimizing false negatives is the clinical priority, sensitivity remains the more directly relevant single metric, with F1 serving as a complementary summary of overall precision-recall balance.

The mean Youden Index of 0.413 (95\% CI: 0.370--0.456, STD = 0.138) was the most variable metric in relative terms, consistent with its dependence on both sensitivity and specificity, the latter being inherently unstable in imbalanced settings. Nevertheless, a Youden Index above 0.37 across the full confidence interval confirms that the model operates meaningfully above chance discrimination at the selected thresholds. The relatively wide CI of the Youden Index further underscores that composite balance metrics are more partition-sensitive than sensitivity-dominant metrics in this class distribution, reinforcing the rationale for prioritizing AUPRC and sensitivity as primary evaluation criteria for this use case.

Taken together, these repeated cross-validation results establish that CatBoost's performance across the pipeline's 4-fold structure is statistically stable and not the product of favorable data splitting. This reproducibility is a prerequisite for treating the model as a credible decision-support component; it is not, on its own, evidence of readiness for clinical deployment, which would additionally require external validation (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). With this overall picture established, we next turn to the specific four fold models selected to represent this pipeline throughout the remainder of this Discussion.

\subsubsection{Model Selection, Performance, and Clinical Suitability for Pre-Diagnostic Screening}
%==================================================================================================
The repeated cross-validation above characterizes CatBoost's performance in aggregate, but the Explainability and Counterfactual Generation analyses that follow require concrete, trained models rather than an abstract average across 40 partitions: SHAP attributions and DiCE counterfactuals are computed against one specific fitted CatBoost ensemble per fold, and no meaningful ``average model'' can be constructed by averaging 40 independently grown tree ensembles with 40 different tuned hyperparameter sets. Some single repeat's four fold models must therefore be selected and saved for reuse, without refitting, by every subsequent stage of this Discussion---and, as reported below, all downstream analysis of feature importance, SHAP explanations, and counterfactual generation is dependent on whichever repeat is chosen for this purpose.

We use the first of the ten repeats (fixed by the pipeline's random seed, Section~\ref{sec:hpo}) as this representative set of four fold models. Two considerations defend this choice. First, it is fixed by the pipeline's design rather than selected after comparing repeats' outcomes, so it cannot reflect cherry-picking a favorably-performing repeat; the same repeat would have been used regardless of how it happened to score. Second, its representativeness is directly supported by the concordance with the pooled 40-repeat estimates above: as the discussion below shows, the first repeat's per-fold mean estimates (Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}) fall within roughly one standard deviation of the full 40-repeat pooled estimates (Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats}) for every metric, indicating that this particular repeat is not an atypical draw from the pipeline's overall performance distribution. This second point should nonetheless be read as a plausibility check rather than a guarantee: it confirms the first repeat's \emph{aggregate} behavior is unremarkable, not that every individual fold within it is equally representative---Model 0's comparatively low sensitivity, discussed below, is one place where this repeat does show fold-specific behavior worth keeping in mind when interpreting the Explainability and Counterfactual results that build on it.

Table \ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat} shows the best parameters and performance metrics for the four models, together with their mean and standard deviation. Mean specificity (0.561) and F1 (0.842) are modest improvements over the baseline unoptimized CatBoost model (0.512 and 0.858, see Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics}), while mean sensitivity (0.870) is slightly below the unoptimized baseline (0.913)---a direct consequence of per-fold Bayesian threshold optimization trading some sensitivity for a better-balanced operating point rather than maximizing recall alone. For a pre-diagnostic screening instrument intended to triage patients toward the more resource-intensive and often geographically inaccessible Nerve Conduction Study (NCS), the clinical priority remains high sensitivity, so this trade-off is examined further at the pipeline level in the repeated cross-validation analysis above.

Per-fold thresholds were tightly clustered (0.483--0.500, a range of just 0.017), with each fold's Bayesian search converging on a similar operating point near 0.49, so this narrow range cannot explain the considerable spread in mean specificity (0.561, range: 0.357--0.643). That spread more plausibly reflects two compounding sources of per-fold variability instead: which Unconfirmed-DPN patients happened to fall into each fold's held-out test set, and the substantially different hyperparameters each fold's independent search converged on---most notably the positive-class weight, which ranged from 0.633 (Model 3) to 0.954 (Model 2) and directly governs how the model trades sensitivity against specificity (Table~\ref{tab:catboost_metrics_firstrepeat}).

Variability in Youden Index across folds (0.297 to 0.549) highlights that Model 3 achieved the most balanced sensitivity-specificity operating point among the four, potentially attributable to the particular composition of its training partition. Nevertheless, sensitivity remained above 0.90 for three of the four folds (Model 1: 0.939, Models 2 and 3: 0.906), with Model 0 the outlier at 0.727---a fold-specific weak point that the repeated cross-validation analysis above and the ensemble-level counterfactual discussion (Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual}) both help contextualize. The use of 4-fold stratified cross-validation---rather than a single train-test split---ensured that every patient contributed to model evaluation, reducing selection bias and providing a more reliable estimate of out-of-sample performance for a dataset of this size \cite{steyerberg2010assessing}.

The F1-scores, ranging from 0.774 to 0.879 (mean: 0.842), and AUPRC values ranging from 0.837 to 0.941 (mean: 0.893), indicate that all four fold models substantially exceed the no-skill baseline of 0.695 (Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics}), with CatBoost and gradient-boosting approaches generally recognized for handling mixed feature types and class imbalance without extensive preprocessing---properties especially beneficial in clinical tabular data \cite{dorogush2018catboost}.

\subsection{Feature Importance and Model Interpretability}
%*********************************************************
Across all four folds, CatBoost feature importances (Figure~\ref{fig:feature_importances}) and SHAP-based explanations (Figure~\ref{fig:shap}) converged on a consistent set of clinically meaningful predictors, reinforcing the model's interpretability and lending biological plausibility to its decision logic---a critical consideration for clinical acceptance of machine learning tools \cite{rudin2019}.

Five predictors occupied the upper tier of the importance ranking in every fold, although their relative order varied from fold to fold: decreased ankle reflex (DEC\_AR; importance 8.35--13.82, SHAP 0.047--0.079), diabetes mellitus duration (DM\_DUR; importance 7.71--11.62, SHAP 0.017--0.059), feet Sudoscan asymmetry (FEET\_PCT\_ASYM; importance 7.02--11.74, SHAP 0.010--0.048), decreased vibration sensation (DEC\_VS; importance 4.64--9.81, SHAP 0.019--0.068), and the MNSI score (importance 5.35--10.55, SHAP 0.013--0.047). Only DEC\_AR placed among the three most important features in all four folds (ranks 2, 3, 3, 1 in Models 0--3), with DM\_DUR next most stable (ranks 4, 1, 1, 3). The remaining three were substantially more fold-dependent: FEET\_PCT\_ASYM (ranks 1, 6, 2, 6), DEC\_VS (ranks 3, 4, 5, 12), and MNSI (ranks 9, 2, 7, 2), the last reaching an importance of 10.55 in Model 1---behind only DM\_DUR's 11.62 in that fold, and ahead of DEC\_AR's 9.87. The consistent membership of this group, rather than any fixed ordering within it, is what the four folds agree on. This is clinically plausible: loss of ankle deep tendon reflex and impaired vibration perception are among the earliest and most reliable large-fiber neurological signs of DPN, and long-standing hyperglycemia (indexed here by diabetes duration) is a well-established driver of cumulative nerve damage \cite{tesfaye2010}. Their consistent presence in the upper tier across all four independently trained folds indicates that the model is anchoring its discriminative reasoning on diagnostically recognizable clinical signs rather than idiosyncratic artifacts of any single training partition.

Beyond this top group, the Sudoscan-derived cluster contributed substantial predictive weight in aggregate: the neuropathy score (NS; importance 6.68--7.88, SHAP 0.021--0.042), hand Sudoscan asymmetry (HAND\_PCT\_ASYM; importance 5.27--7.52, SHAP 0.008--0.032), and the cardiac autonomic score (CAS; importance 5.52--6.84, SHAP 0.014--0.017) each ranked in the upper half of the feature set in every fold, with foot- and hand-level mean electrochemical skin conductance (FEET\_MEAN\_ESC, HAND\_MEAN\_ESC) contributing more moderately. Sudoscan measures small-fiber sudomotor function through galvanic skin response and has been used as a rapid, noninvasive surrogate marker of sudomotor neuropathy, an early manifestation of diabetic small-fiber involvement that frequently precedes or accompanies the large-fiber involvement detectable by NCS \cite{casellini2013sudoscan}. The co-prominence of large-fiber neurological examination findings, diabetes duration, and multiple Sudoscan-derived small-fiber parameters across all folds suggests the model is integrating complementary information spanning both large- and small-fiber neuropathy pathways, rather than relying on a single modality---consistent with the finding at the unoptimized-baseline stage that the SudoNeuro feature combination alone recovered nearly full-feature-set performance (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_heatmaps}).

HbA1c (HBA1C; importance 5.21--8.13, SHAP 0.007--0.025), age (AGE; importance 5.51--6.48, SHAP 0.014--0.028), and insulin use (INSULIN; importance 1.08--2.58, SHAP 0.004--0.009) contributed consistently but at lower magnitudes than the top predictors above. This is a point of direct relevance to the counterfactual analysis that follows (Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual}): HbA1c and insulin use are comparatively modest drivers of the model's overall feature importance, yet---because they are the features most amenable to clinical intervention---they dominate the counterfactual ``recipes'' DiCE proposes for reclassification. Feature importance and counterfactual salience answer different questions (what the model relies on across the whole cohort, versus what can be changed for a given patient) and are not expected to rank features identically.

In contrast, the comorbidities hypertension (HPN) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) received consistently low feature importance and SHAP values across all four folds, while peripheral arterial occlusive disease (PAOD) and Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) received near-zero importance in every fold, suggesting these comorbidities do not independently contribute much to DPN prediction in this cohort---a finding that likely reflects their comparatively low prevalence in the study population and their pathophysiological distinction from diabetic neuropathy itself.

As introduced above, this analysis---not the classifier's raw discriminative performance---is the central contribution of this study.

\paragraph{Feature Actionability and the Counterfactual Candidate Pool}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------
A prerequisite for clinically meaningful counterfactuals is that the features being altered must correspond to interventions that are realistically achievable. Accordingly, features were stratified by actionability prior to counterfactual generation (Table~\ref{tab:cf_features}). Immutable demographic attributes (sex, age, DM duration) and examination-derived measurements (neurological findings, MNSI, Sudoscan readings) were excluded, as these do not correspond to modifiable patient states. This exclusion is not merely a technical constraint: counterfactuals generated from non-actionable features risk producing explanations that are statistically valid but clinically misleading \cite{karimi2020}. The final actionable feature set---HbA1c, insulin use, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CKD, and PAOD---reflects a pragmatic and evidence-aligned subset of DPN risk factors for which clinical pathways exist. As the case studies below illustrate, however, being on this actionable list is necessary but not sufficient for a specific counterfactual to be a realistic clinical recommendation: DiCE is free to propose \emph{introducing} a comorbidity such as PAOD or CKD (an actionable feature can move in either direction) even though inducing a new disease is never a real intervention, so each proposed change still requires clinical judgment about its direction, not just its feature identity. Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading} in Section~\ref{sec:instance-level-cf} sets out how that judgment is made for each type of change, and shows that it turns further on which way the counterfactual flips the prediction.

\paragraph{Candidate Instances and Counterfactual Generation}
%------------------------------------------------------------
\label{sec:cf-plausibility}
We identified misclassified instances (incorrect predictions) and borderline instances (prediction probability within 0.08 of the fold's own decision threshold) as candidate instances for counterfactual generation. A total of 61 candidates were identified across the four folds (42 misclassified, 19 borderline; Table~\ref{tab:localcf-model-level}), but only 8 yielded a usable counterfactual within the 1-hour search budget allowed per patient---none of them from Model 2, whose 12 candidates all failed to converge. Table~\ref{tab:localcf-model-level} provides a summary of the counterfactuals generated per model while Table~\ref{tab:localcf-listing} provides details of the 8 candidates that successfully generated counterfactuals.

This low yield is a direct, documented consequence of how DiCE's genetic search behaves on this dataset, not an arbitrary generation failure, and it is worth being explicit about the configuration and its limits so that the counterfactuals reported below are read with the right degree of confidence. Counterfactuals were generated with DiCE's diversity-seeking genetic search (\texttt{DiverseCF}), targeting up to 20 diverse counterfactuals per patient, with proximity weight 0.5, diversity weight 1.0, a categorical penalty of 0.1, and posthoc sparsity pruning (binary search, parameter 0.05) applied for maximally sparse, clinically legible output. Separately from the yield problem, the genetic search on this dataset and schema has an independently documented tendency to occasionally violate the immutability constraint on the features permitted to vary (Table~\ref{tab:local_cf})---i.e., to propose changing a feature explicitly marked non-actionable, such as SUBJ or a Sudoscan reading---in a small fraction of counterfactuals for a given patient, regardless of model or configuration; every reported counterfactual in this study has been screened against this constraint and any violating counterfactual removed before reporting, and the removed counterfactuals are reported separately, for two of the three case studies below, as a transparency record rather than silently discarded. Both behaviors---the outright non-convergence for an entire fold, and the intermittent constraint violations within an otherwise successful run---stem from the same underlying cause: a genetic search's fitness landscape can be poorly conditioned in some regions of a small, high-dimensional clinical feature space, and CatBoost's smoother predicted-probability surface (Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf}) mitigates, but does not eliminate, this fragility. In practice, this means the 8 patients discussed below are not a representative sample of ``typical'' DPN cases; they are the subset for which the search happened to succeed, and the absence of a counterfactual for any other candidate is evidence of search difficulty, not evidence that no clinically meaningful counterfactual exists for that patient.

\subsubsection{Instance Level Counterfactual Analysis}
%=====================================================
\label{sec:instance-level-cf}
In this section we examine three of the eight successful instances individually, chosen to span the three clinically distinct scenarios a screening tool can produce: a borderline false positive, a borderline true positive, and a confident false negative. Unlike the model's decision threshold or feature importances, these are patient-specific, so we deliberately draw the three cases from three different fold models (3, 0, and 1 respectively) rather than confining the discussion to a single fold. The counterfactuals generated for these instances are shown together in Figure~\ref{fig:localcf-cases}. The remaining five successful instances (Patients 29, 76, 125, 23, 172; Table~\ref{tab:localcf-listing}) are not discussed individually here; their counterfactuals are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:s5} (Appendix) and enter the aggregate analysis in Section~\ref{sec:aggregate-cf} below.

Before turning to the individual cases, it is worth stating explicitly how a counterfactual for this task should be read, because these three cases differ along two independent axes and conflating them is the likeliest source of misinterpretation.

The first axis is the \emph{direction of the flip}, which fixes the register in which a proposed change can be discussed at all. When a counterfactual flips a prediction to Unconfirmed DPN, the target state is one a clinician would welcome, so each proposed change can legitimately be examined as a candidate action: is it something that can be done to a patient, and should it be? When a counterfactual flips a prediction to Confirmed DPN, no proposed change can be an action at all---no clinician intervenes in order to give a patient neuropathy---and the only available reading is evidential: what does the change reveal about the associations the model has learned, and are those associations clinically plausible? Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading} sets out how each type of feature change reads under each of the two directions.

The second axis is whether the prediction being flipped was \emph{correct}, which fixes what the counterfactual set is for. When the prediction is wrong, the flip moves toward the clinical truth and the set diagnoses a model error---the evidence that produced a false alarm, or the evidence whose absence produced a miss. When the prediction is right, the flip moves away from the clinical truth and the set instead probes robustness, measuring how securely the model holds a decision it got correct. The two axes are independent, and their independence is easy to overlook: a change can be entirely sensible as a clinical act and still, for a correctly classified patient, describe an outcome nobody wants, because what it achieves is the removal of a true alarm. The three cases below occupy three of the four resulting combinations; the fourth---a correctly predicted negative flipped toward Confirmed DPN---has no instance among our eight and appears in Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading} only for completeness.

Two further rules apply throughout. First, direction is a necessary but not a sufficient screen: a change may point the expected way and still be implausible in magnitude, so the size of the proposed perturbation must be judged separately from its sign. Second, the classification applies to a counterfactual as a whole and is gated by its weakest element---a counterfactual pairing a sensible HbA1c reduction with the introduction of a new comorbidity is not partly usable but wholly unusable, because the patient cannot arrive at the counterfactual state without both changes.

\paragraph{Patient 20---Borderline False Positive (Model 3)}
%-------------------------------------------------------------
\textit{Patient Profile}: Female, 51 years old; diabetes duration 1 year; HbA1c 10.3\%; on insulin; no hypertension or PAOD; dyslipidemia present; no CKD. Predicted as Confirmed DPN at a probability of 0.498, a hair above Model 3's threshold of 0.492 (margin = 0.006)---essentially a coin flip---despite an unconfirmed clinical diagnosis. Her counterfactuals are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:localcf-cases}a.

Because Patient 20 is a false positive, her counterfactuals flip the prediction \emph{toward} the clinically correct label: they describe what the model would have needed to see in order not to raise a false alarm, and are therefore read here as a diagnosis of the model's error rather than as advice for the patient. With a margin of only 0.006, Patient 20 sits closer to her fold's decision boundary than any other candidate in this study, and the 22 counterfactuals generated for her reflect that: this is the most parsimonious case in the dataset, with a mean sparsity of 1.82 features (Table~\ref{tab:localcf-listing}). Several counterfactuals require changing only a single actionable feature: stopping insulin alone (INSULIN: 1$\to$0), managing dyslipidemia alone (DSLPDMIA: 1$\to$0), or reducing HbA1c alone by as little as 2.3--2.7 points. Two-feature combinations are similarly small---for example, stopping insulin together with a 0.2--0.3 point HbA1c reduction. Across all 22 counterfactuals, HbA1c is the most frequently changed feature (16 of 22), followed by insulin (11 of 22) and dyslipidemia (7 of 22); hypertension, PAOD, and CKD changes are rare (2, 1, and 3 instances respectively). One counterfactual moves the immutable feature DM\_DUR alongside HbA1c and was filtered out as unactionable (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}) rather than reported as a valid recommendation. Because the margin is so small and the counterfactuals so sparse, this case shows precisely which evidence tipped the model into a false alarm: a markedly elevated HbA1c of 10.3\% in a patient with only 1 year of diabetes duration, reinforced by insulin use and dyslipidemia. Withdrawing any one of those three is enough to reverse the call, which identifies them as the drivers of the misclassification. Read against Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading}, however, those three changes do not all fall the same way. Reducing an HbA1c of 10.3\% and bringing dyslipidemia under control are both enactable and advisable; stopping insulin---appearing in 11 of the 22 counterfactuals---is enactable but inadvisable, and would be clinically inappropriate to act on for a patient with this HbA1c. Because Patient 20 does not in fact have confirmed DPN, the advisable subset is at least harmless: acting on it costs her nothing she would not have been advised to do anyway. That is a consequence of her being a false positive, though, not a warrant carried by the counterfactual, and the very same changes proposed for a correctly flagged patient would carry the opposite implication (Patient 40 below). What the counterfactual itself contributes is transparency, telling the clinician why the screening tool flagged a patient whose DPN was not confirmed.

\paragraph{Patient 40---Borderline True Positive (Model 0)}
%------------------------------------------------------------
\textit{Patient Profile}: Female, 62 years old; diabetes duration 5 years; HbA1c 7.5\%; on insulin; hypertension, PAOD, and dyslipidemia present; no CKD. Correctly predicted as Confirmed DPN at a probability of 0.513, just above Model 0's threshold of 0.499 (margin = 0.014), matching the confirmed clinical diagnosis. Her counterfactuals are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:localcf-cases}b.

Patient 40 inverts the situation: because she is a true positive, her counterfactuals flip the prediction \emph{away} from the clinically correct label, so they cannot be read as a route to a better outcome at all. What they measure is the stability of a correct decision---how much supporting evidence would have to be withdrawn before the model changed its mind. The 24 generated counterfactuals show what it would take to move her below the threshold. HbA1c is changed in every single one of the 24 counterfactuals, the only patient in this study for which that is true, and the single most parsimonious counterfactual overall is a 0.5-point HbA1c reduction (7.5\% $\to$ 7.0\%) with no other change---the smallest single-feature perturbation observed for any patient (L1 = 0.5). Two-feature combinations largely pair a similar HbA1c reduction with stopping insulin (18 of 24 counterfactuals touch insulin) or resolving PAOD (6 of 24); hypertension and CKD are altered less often (4 and 2 instances). Two counterfactuals instead moved the immutable SUBJ feature alongside a larger HbA1c reduction and were filtered out as unactionable. Her set also separates cleanly under Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading}, and it does so in a way that makes the two axes visibly independent. Eighteen of the 24 counterfactuals involve stopping insulin, which is enactable but inadvisable on its own terms: withdrawing indicated therapy from a patient with established diabetes is not a recommendation, and those counterfactuals are unusable without any appeal at all to her having been classified correctly. The most parsimonious proposal is the opposite case. A 0.5-point HbA1c reduction is enactable and advisable, exactly the kind of change standard diabetes care already pursues, and yet its effect on the model is one nobody would want, because it removes the flag from a patient whose DPN is confirmed. The majority of this set fails as advice on clinical grounds, while its best-behaved member fails on the grounds of what it accomplishes. Because this patient is both correctly classified and borderline, then, the counterfactual does not correct an error; it measures the fragility of a correct one. A 0.5-point HbA1c reduction would not reverse a confirmed DPN diagnosis, but it is enough to move this patient from flagged to unflagged, which is a caution about how little evidence separates the two classes near the threshold rather than a reassurance about her prognosis. The practical implication is for the screening tool, not the care plan: correct positives this close to the boundary should be treated as provisional and confirmed by clinical examination.

\paragraph{Patient 123---Confident False Negative (Model 1)}
%-------------------------------------------------------------
\textit{Patient Profile}: Female, 68 years old; diabetes duration 3 years; HbA1c 7.18\%; not on insulin; hypertension present; no PAOD, dyslipidemia, or CKD. Incorrectly predicted as Unconfirmed DPN at a probability of 0.269, well below Model 1's threshold of 0.5 (margin = 0.231), despite a confirmed clinical diagnosis. Her counterfactuals are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:localcf-cases}c.

Patient 123 is a false negative, so, as with Patient 20, her counterfactuals flip the prediction \emph{toward} the clinically correct label and describe a model deficit---here, the evidence the model would have needed in order to catch a patient it missed. She is the only false negative among the eight successful instances and, at a margin of 0.231, the least borderline case in this study---Model 1 places her confidently in the wrong class. The 24 counterfactuals generated for her therefore describe what would need to be different for the model to correctly flag her as Confirmed DPN, and every one of them changes HbA1c downward (typically by 1.2--2.2 points, e.g., 7.18\% $\to$ 5.0\%), the largest and most consistent single driver of any patient in this study; hypertension is the second most frequent change (removed in 15 of 24), followed by PAOD (introduced in 11 of 24), insulin (started in 7 of 24), dyslipidemia (introduced in 2 of 24), and CKD (introduced in 1 of 24). The directions of these changes deserve careful reading, and Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading} separates them into two groups that say quite different things. Because this flip targets Confirmed DPN, none of them is an intervention proposal under any reading---no clinician acts in order to make a patient neuropathic---so the only question available is whether the association each change implies is clinically plausible. The comorbidity introductions fall on the plausible side: PAOD, dyslipidemia, and CKD are all recognized DPN risk factors, so a model that raises its probability in their presence has learned something clinically coherent, even though introducing them is not something that can be done to a patient. Starting insulin is likewise hypothesis-generating rather than aberrant, consistent with insulin use marking longer-standing or poorer-controlled diabetes rather than causing neuropathy. The HbA1c and hypertension changes are the ones that do not survive. Every counterfactual lowers her HbA1c and 15 of 24 remove her hypertension---including the smallest of them, HbA1c $-$1.78 combined with resolving hypertension---which implies that better glycemic control and treated blood pressure \emph{raise} the probability of DPN, the reverse of the established risk direction, and is certainly not an argument for relaxing either. Magnitude compounds the problem: a shift from 7.18\% to 5.0\% is not merely inverted in sign but larger than a plausible clinical trajectory for this patient. The set therefore describes a mixed picture rather than a uniform failure. Within this training partition, Model 1 has learned a defensible comorbidity signal alongside an inverted glycemic and blood-pressure one, and it is the inverted component that dominates her counterfactuals and that leaves her actual profile---HbA1c 7.18\% with hypertension and no other comorbidity---on the unconfirmed side. No counterfactual was screened out as unactionable for this patient, because every altered feature here happens to fall on the actionable list (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}); the actionable/non-actionable distinction still has to be applied at the level of the specific proposed change (removing a condition versus introducing one), not merely at the level of which feature was touched. Clinically, this is the case that most directly illustrates the stakes of a false negative in this screening framework: a patient with confirmed DPN, comfortably below the referral threshold, for whom the counterfactual does not indicate what should be done but rather exposes where the model's learned associations diverge from clinical ones---the feature values it would have needed to see, in directions that carry no therapeutic reading, before recognizing the confirmed-DPN phenotype she actually has.

\paragraph{Synthesis of the Three Case Studies}
%----------------------------------------------
\label{sec:cf-synthesis}
Read together, these three cases occupy three of the four cells of the framework set out at the head of this section, and each cell reads differently. Patients 20 and 123 were predicted incorrectly, so their flips move toward the clinical truth and their counterfactuals diagnose a model error---the evidence that produced a false alarm in the first case, the evidence whose absence produced a miss in the second. Patient 40 was predicted correctly, so her flip moves away from the clinical truth and her counterfactuals probe robustness instead, measuring how securely the model holds a decision it got right. Crossing this against the direction of the flip explains why the same feature carries different weight in different cases. For Patients 20 and 40 the flip targets Unconfirmed DPN, so each proposed change can be examined as a candidate action; for Patient 123 it targets Confirmed DPN, so no proposed change is an action at all and the set can only be read as evidence about what the model has learned.

The three cases also show that the two axes cut across each other rather than lining up. Reducing HbA1c is enactable and advisable in both flip-to-negative cases, and yet it is benign for Patient 20, whose DPN is not confirmed, and unwelcome for Patient 40, where the identical change would unflag a patient whose DPN is. Stopping insulin runs the other way: it is the most frequent change after HbA1c in both (11 of 22 and 18 of 24 counterfactuals) and is inadvisable in both, independently of whether the underlying prediction was correct. The clinical desirability of a change and the usefulness of the counterfactual containing it are separate judgments, and either can fail while the other holds.

Sparsity, for its part, tracks the margin as expected. For Patient 20 and Patient 40, both borderline (margins of 0.006 and 0.014), the counterfactuals are small, single- or two-feature perturbations dominated by HbA1c---exactly what ``minimal sufficient change'' should look like in a case that is genuinely close to the threshold. For Patient 123, confidently misclassified (margin 0.231), the set is necessarily larger and more mixed, and it divides along the plausibility line rather than the size line: her comorbidity introductions and her insulin change imply associations that are clinically coherent, whereas her HbA1c and hypertension changes imply inverted ones. That split is more informative than any verdict on the set as a whole would be, because it localizes the deficit to specific features instead of dismissing the model's reasoning wholesale.

Across all three cases HbA1c is the feature the model is most sensitive to, echoing its central role in the aggregate-level analysis below (Section~\ref{sec:aggregate-cf}); insulin and, in Patient 123's case, hypertension are the next most influential. The artifacts a human reader must filter out recur in three kinds: changes that cannot be enacted at all (introducing a comorbidity, or moving an immutable feature), changes that could be enacted but should not be (stopping insulin), and changes whose implied association is inverted (Patient 123's HbA1c and hypertension). Because a counterfactual state is reached only if all of its changes occur together, a single member of any of these three kinds disqualifies the entire counterfactual rather than just the offending feature. This is precisely the caveat raised in Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}: a generated counterfactual is a candidate explanation to be clinically reviewed, not an automatic prescription.

HbA1c modification is the single most frequently proposed change across the aggregate counterfactual set (144 of 158, 91.1\%), but the pattern is multifactorial rather than HbA1c-only: insulin (81, 51.3\%) and dyslipidemia (59, 37.3\%) are substantial secondary levers, not minor contributors, and the mean sparsity of 2.16--2.65 features per counterfactual (Table~\ref{tab:localcf-model-level}) means an HbA1c change is almost always accompanied by a second. This is clinically coherent and well-supported in the literature: chronic hyperglycemia---indexed by HbA1c---drives the oxidative and metabolic mechanisms underlying peripheral nerve damage in diabetic patients, and tighter glycemic control has been demonstrated to reduce DPN incidence and progression \cite{callaghan2012,tesfaye2010}. The counterfactual analysis, thus, not only identifies HbA1c as the most frequently implicated actionable feature, but operationalizes this importance as a concrete clinical recommendation: patients flagged as high-risk by the model may be redirected toward glycemic optimization, insulin therapy review, and dyslipidemia management, in roughly that order of frequency, before or alongside a Nerve Conduction Study referral.

Model-level heterogeneity (Figure~\ref{fig:globalcf}) reinforces a point already made about the selection and optimization stages above: each fold is a different sample of the same 187-patient cohort, and its counterfactual ``signature'' reflects the specific patients who happened to be borderline or misclassified in that fold, not four independent findings to be reconciled. Model 1's hypertension/PAOD-led pattern, for instance, is a description of Patient 123 specifically (Section~\ref{sec:instance-level-cf}), not evidence that hypertension generally outranks HbA1c in this cohort.

Combined with the feature-frequency pattern above, this suggests the models rely on a small number of co-occurring risk factors---most often HbA1c together with one of insulin, dyslipidemia, or hypertension---rather than a single feature threshold or the full comorbidity profile, a pattern consistent with the known multifactorial pathogenesis of DPN \cite{feldman2019}. Dyslipidemia and hypertension's substantial representation (59 and 49 instances respectively, Table~\ref{tab:localcf-changed}) is consistent with their established roles as peripheral neuropathy risk amplifiers through vascular and metabolic mechanisms \cite{jaiswal2017,callaghan2012}.

In terms of distance from the reference instance, Model 1 (represented only by Patient 123) has the highest mean L1/L2 distance among the three successful models (3.40/2.29), consistent with that patient's large decision margin (0.231) requiring a larger perturbation to cross; Model 3 produced the most parsimonious counterfactuals overall (sparsity mean 2.16, L1 mean 2.95), consistent with Patients 20 and 172 both being close to their threshold.

The integration of counterfactual analysis into this pre-diagnostic screening framework addresses a critical barrier to ML adoption in clinical settings: interpretability. Black-box predictions, however accurate, are unlikely to be acted upon by clinicians without a justification that aligns with their clinical reasoning \cite{rudin2019}. By grounding model explanations in actionable, modifiable features and framing them as ``what-if'' scenarios consistent with clinical intervention pathways, the counterfactual approach offers a practical bridge between statistical prediction and clinical decision-making---provided, as emphasized throughout this section, that each proposed change is still read by a clinician rather than acted on automatically. Notably, the approach does not require clinicians to understand the internal mechanics of the CatBoost model; instead, it presents outputs in a form familiar to clinical reasoning: ``If this patient's HbA1c and insulin regimen were better controlled, the model would not flag them for NCS referral.''

This has implications not only for individual patient management but for population-level screening program design, as the most frequently changed features across all counterfactuals---HbA1c, insulin use, and dyslipidemia---directly correspond to the targets of existing diabetes complication management guidelines \cite{ada2024}.

\subsection{A Proposed Clinical Usage Workflow}
%**********************************************
The case studies and aggregate patterns above suggest a concrete, low-burden way this pipeline could fit into an existing diabetes clinic workflow, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:clinician_workflow}. At a routine follow-up visit, the clinic already collects (or can readily collect) the profile, comorbidity, MNSI, and brief neurological examination data this model uses, optionally supplemented by Sudoscan; no Nerve Conduction Study is required to generate a risk score. The CatBoost model then produces a predicted probability of Confirmed DPN against its fold-calibrated threshold. For patients whose prediction is comfortably below threshold, no further action is needed beyond routine follow-up. For patients whose predicted probability sits well above the threshold, the flag is already high-confidence and the patient is prioritized for Nerve Conduction Study referral without counterfactual review. For patients who are borderline---flagged, but close to the threshold---the corresponding DiCE counterfactual is reviewed alongside the prediction: it identifies the smallest realistic change, among the six actionable features, that would move the patient across the threshold. As Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility} and the case studies in Section~\ref{sec:instance-level-cf} make clear, this review step requires a clinician to filter the suggestion. Because the patients reaching this step are ones the model has flagged, their counterfactuals flip toward Unconfirmed DPN and can be read in the action register of Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading}: the clinician discards any proposal that cannot be enacted (introducing a new comorbidity, or altering an immutable attribute) or that could be enacted but should not be (withdrawing indicated insulin therapy), and evaluates the remainder---typically glycemic control, or management of hypertension and dyslipidemia---against the patient's broader clinical picture. The case studies add one caution to this step: a flagged patient may be flagged correctly, in which case an otherwise sensible proposal is a warning about how little evidence separates the classes near the threshold, not a care plan. The output of that review then supports one of three actions. Where the review leaves few or no actionable pathways, the patient joins those flagged well above the threshold in being prioritized for Nerve Conduction Study referral. Where actionable pathways do remain, the clinician judges whether any of them corresponds to an intervention that can realistically be made for this patient: if so, the patient is directed toward targeted risk-factor management and re-screened at a subsequent visit; if not, the patient is directed toward general risk-factor management and held at moderate priority for reassessment rather than referred immediately.

We emphasize, as throughout this Discussion, that this workflow is a proposal for how the pipeline's outputs could support clinical decision-making, not a validated clinical protocol: the risk score and the counterfactual are decision-support inputs, and the referral or treatment decision remains the clinician's.

\subsection{Limitations}
%***********************
\label{sec:limitations}
Beyond the stage-specific limitations already noted (Section~\ref{sec:models}: baseline evaluation; repeated cross-validation above: model stability), several limitations apply to the study as a whole and should temper how these results are read.

The data come from a single clinic and a single patient cohort (n = 187). Even with repeated stratified cross-validation, this cannot substitute for external validation on an independent cohort, and the reported performance and counterfactual patterns may not generalize to populations with different demographics, comorbidity prevalence, or diagnostic practices. Relatedly, the cohort was not randomly sampled from a defined population, so some degree of selection bias---for instance, toward patients already referred for the neurological work-up that established their DPN status---cannot be ruled out.

This study is a proof-of-concept decision-support investigation, not a validated or deployment-ready clinical tool. No prospective testing has been performed: the model and its counterfactuals have not been evaluated in a live clinical workflow, and it is not known whether clinicians presented with these outputs would make different or better referral decisions than they would otherwise. Deploying a screening model of this kind carries real risks if these steps are skipped---a false negative could delay a diagnosis, and a false positive could prompt an unnecessary referral or, more subtly, undue reassurance to a clinician who over-trusts a favorable counterfactual---which is precisely why we frame this work as decision support requiring clinician oversight at every step (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}), rather than as an autonomous diagnostic tool.

Finally, the small dataset size, while addressed methodologically through repeated cross-validation and CatBoost's regularization properties (Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf}), remains a fundamental constraint: it limits the granularity of subgroup analysis, contributes to the specificity instability documented above, and is the same underlying reason a subset of counterfactual candidates could not be resolved by the DiCE genetic search (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}).

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Conclusions may be used to restate your hypothesis or research question, restate your major findings, explain the relevance and the added value of your work, highlight any limitations of your study, describe future directions for research and recommendations. 

This study asked two connected questions. First, whether a machine learning model trained only on data a diabetes clinic can obtain without electrodiagnostic equipment---clinical profile, comorbidities, MNSI, a brief bedside neurological examination, and Sudoscan---can identify patients with a high probability of confirmed diabetic peripheral neuropathy and prioritize them for nerve conduction study. Second, and more centrally, whether counterfactual explanations can convert that prediction into something a clinician can act on, rather than merely describe. The framing throughout has been triage toward the diagnostic reference standard in a setting where that standard is costly and often geographically inaccessible, not replacement of it; accordingly, nerve conduction study measurements were excluded from every predictor set at every stage and retained solely as the label they define.

On a cohort of 187 Filipino patients with diabetes (130 Confirmed, 57 Unconfirmed DPN), a benchmark of fourteen algorithms across twelve feature subsets under 10-repeat $\times$ 4-fold stratified cross-validation showed that ensemble tree methods clustered at the top and were statistically indistinguishable from one another---Random Forest at 0.892 mean AUPRC, CatBoost at 0.889, Extra Trees at 0.885, against a no-skill baseline of 0.695 (Table~\ref{tab:selection_metrics}). CatBoost was carried forward not on that ranking, which cannot separate the three, but on two properties the ranking cannot express: its ordered boosting and symmetric tree structure constrain the hypothesis space in a way suited to a 187-patient cohort, and its additive, sigmoid-transformed output gives DiCE's genetic search a smoother probability surface to climb than a vote-share ensemble would (Section~\ref{sec:catboost_vs_rf}). After nested cross-validated Bayesian optimization, the pipeline achieved a mean AUPRC of 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.871--0.902) and a mean sensitivity of 0.886 (95\% CI: 0.861--0.912) across 40 train/test splits (Table~\ref{tab:optimization_metrics_40repeats})---reproducible across partitions and weighted toward the metric that matters most when the cost of a miss is a delayed diagnosis. Specificity was the one unstable metric (mean 0.527, 95\% CI: 0.483--0.571), reflecting the smaller Unconfirmed class and per-fold variation in tuned class weights, and it remains the pipeline's clearest performance weakness.

Feature-set ablation produced a result with immediate practical value: bedside neurological examination findings were the least redundant feature group, their removal causing the largest degradation among the top models, while the Sudoscan-plus-neurological-examination combination recovered to within 0.01 AUPRC of the full feature set (Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_heatmaps}). A reduced two-modality panel is therefore a plausible lower-burden screening configuration where a full profile, comorbidity, and MNSI workup is impractical.

CatBoost feature importance and SHAP attributions converged, across all four independently trained fold models, on decreased ankle reflex, diabetes duration, feet Sudoscan asymmetry, decreased vibration sensation, and the MNSI score, with the remaining Sudoscan-derived parameters contributing substantial weight in aggregate (Section~\ref{sec:explainability}). This is biologically coherent---the model integrates large-fiber examination signs, cumulative hyperglycemic exposure, and small-fiber sudomotor measures rather than leaning on a single modality. It is also, however, the precise point at which importance-based explanation stops being useful to a clinician: every one of those top predictors is a measurement of established neuropathic state, not a lever anyone can pull. Counterfactual analysis exists in this study to close that gap.

Restricted to the six features for which a clinical pathway exists---HbA1c, insulin use, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and peripheral arterial occlusive disease---DiCE produced 158 counterfactuals across 8 patients. HbA1c was the single most frequently altered feature (144 of 158, 91.1\%), but rarely alone: insulin (51.3\%) and dyslipidemia (37.3\%) were substantial secondary levers, followed by hypertension (31.0\%), PAOD (20.3\%) and CKD (13.9\%), and a mean sparsity of 2.16--2.65 features per counterfactual means the typical reversal pairs an HbA1c change with one other actionable feature (Table~\ref{tab:localcf-changed}). The picture is multifactorial rather than glycemia-only, consistent with the known pathogenesis of DPN, and the three features implicated most often map directly onto the targets of existing diabetes complication management guidelines.

Beyond these results, the study's added value lies in three places. It is, to our knowledge, among the first to integrate counterfactual explanation systematically into a DPN pre-diagnostic screening pipeline designed for low-resource settings, and it demonstrates concretely---rather than assuming---that feature importance and counterfactual salience answer different questions and rank features differently. It contributes a reading framework for clinical counterfactuals: the two independent axes of flip direction and prediction correctness, and the action-versus-evidence register they define (Table~\ref{tab:cf-direction-reading}), which generalize beyond DPN to any clinical counterfactual application where a proposed change may be unenactable, inadvisable, or directionally implausible. And it adds geographic and modality diversity to a literature in which Filipino cohorts and Sudoscan-derived predictors are both underrepresented, alongside a concrete proposal for how risk score and counterfactual review might sit inside an existing clinic workflow (Figure~\ref{fig:clinician_workflow}).

These contributions are bounded by limitations that we regard as material rather than pro forma (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The cohort is single-center, modest in size, and not randomly sampled from a defined population, so neither the performance estimates nor the counterfactual patterns can be assumed to transfer; repeated cross-validation reduces partition variance but is not external validation. No prospective evaluation has been performed, and it is unknown whether clinicians shown these outputs would refer differently or better. The counterfactual results carry a further, specific caveat: only 8 of 61 candidate instances yielded a usable counterfactual within the search budget and one entire fold yielded none, so the reported counterfactuals are the subset the genetic search could solve, not a representative sample of DPN patients; the search also intermittently violated its own immutability constraints, requiring post-hoc screening of every reported counterfactual (Section~\ref{sec:cf-plausibility}). Finally, the actionable features are encoded largely as binary comorbidity flags, which limits how finely a proposed change can be translated into a titratable clinical target. For all of these reasons this work is a proof-of-concept decision-support investigation, not a validated or deployment-ready clinical instrument, and every counterfactual it produces is a candidate explanation for clinician review rather than a prescription.

Several directions follow from this. The most urgent is external validation on independent, multi-center and geographically diverse cohorts, followed by prospective clinician-in-the-loop evaluation measuring whether counterfactual-augmented risk scores change referral decisions and improve the diagnostic yield of the nerve conduction studies actually ordered. The low counterfactual yield should be addressed directly, by comparing DiCE's genetic search against alternative generators, by enforcing actionability constraints inside the generator rather than screening for violations afterward, and by testing whether a larger cohort smooths the poorly conditioned regions of the fitness landscape that caused non-convergence. Encoding the actionable features as continuous quantities where they exist---lipid fractions, blood pressure, estimated glomerular filtration rate---rather than as binary comorbidity flags would let counterfactual magnitudes map onto quantitative treatment targets rather than presence or absence of a diagnosis. The reduced Sudoscan-plus-neurological-examination panel deserves prospective evaluation in its own right as the lowest-burden configuration identified here, and specificity stability should be revisited once class sizes permit, since it is the metric that will govern how many unnecessary referrals a deployed screening tool generates. We would also recommend, more generally, that studies applying explainable AI to clinical prediction report explicitly whether their most important features are actionable ones: it is the distinction between explaining a model and supporting a decision, and this study suggests the two require different tools.
